# Chapter 22. WE's League of Legends Captain: Misaya, Yu Jingxi

One day in 2007, Ruofeng argued with his parents about school again. This argument was fiercer than the ones before it, so fierce that he bolted out of the house alone and kept going, a long way from home, down a very long road. The wind was strong that day. As he walked he thought about how good it would be if he could be like the wind: free, unbound, answering to nobody. That is where the handle came from. Ruofeng means like the wind.

Five years later, on October 28, 2012, the captain of the League of Legends team at China's most famous esports club stood with his teammates on the winners' podium at the World e-Sports Masters, WEM. This time he did not cry. He was almost calm. Afterwards he said modestly that the tournament had shown him a great deal that his team still lacked, that they would work on it together, and that they would go after more titles and more honors.

A fighter who had been knocked down and got back up countless times was finally growing up. Before that, like every other esports player of his generation, he had gone through a long stretch of being misunderstood by his family and grinding through hard training.

## Rebellion

Ruofeng was born on October 19, 1992 in Shaodong, Hunan. The family later moved to Xiangtan when his parents' work took them there. His English name is Misaya and his Chinese name is Yu Jingxi, but until he started senior high school he went by a different Chinese name, Yu Jingxiong. His mother came to believe that his strong rebellious streak might have something to do with the name, and that is when it was changed to Yu Jingxi.

An incident at school during his first year of senior high left him deeply sick of the place, to the point that he ran away from home several times. His mother was frantic and, with no better idea, arranged a transfer to another school. Only two months into the new school, he forgot to wear his uniform one day and the head of his year tore into him at the school gate, dwelling in particular on the fact that he was a transfer student, going at him from start to finish without giving him a single chance to explain. That one time ripped his dignity apart. He never went back to that school.

By 2008 Ruofeng was adrift. He liked football, games and literature, but society did not recognize any of that and neither did the examinations, so he lost all interest in school and stopped going.

## His parents' disappointment and his own stubbornness

His father was a university professor who had done postdoctoral work, and had held high expectations for his son from the beginning. During that period his father's disappointment reached its limit. The relationship between father and son came close to breaking down entirely.

His mother, a middle school English teacher, worried herself sick over him. She hoped he would turn around, and at the same time did not dare push too hard in case he ran away again, so she bought a computer to keep him out of the gaming cafes. Those were suffocating months for him. To prove that the road he had chosen was the right one, Ruofeng practiced Dota obsessively at home all day, timing his meals to avoid meeting his parents' eyes, thinking about nothing but how to get stronger. It went on like that for two years.

Carrying that one wish to get stronger, he trained like a madman, sleeping two or three hours a day and watching Dota match replays and tutorials without stopping. Online he got to know Aayaya, captain of the Hunan team Tianma, and his form in practice matches earned him a place as one of their online players. There was a stretch when he swept every prize on offer in Hunan.

## Two failed attempts at turning professional

In 2008 a man in Wuhan said he was putting together a professional team. There were very few professional Dota teams at the time and Ruofeng badly wanted to play professionally, so he took the 1,000 yuan of New Year money he had saved and went. Once he was there the man said he needed to cover rent and had no cash on him. Ruofeng was sixteen. He figured that since he had come all that way he ought to trust the man, so he handed the money over. Then the man disappeared. In the end Ruofeng had to go around borrowing money from people, deeply embarrassed, just to get home.

What surprised him after he got back was that his mother did not scold him. She thought he had gone about the whole thing in earnest and that only the result had turned out badly. From then on she began to encourage him.

That first failure did not stop him. In August 2009 he met the well known Dota player ZSMJ and the two became good friends. One day in October, ZSMJ brought him good news: he wanted Ruofeng to come with him and join FTD, the team being formed by Wu Sheng, the player known by the handle 2009 and later nicknamed the Nine Gods. After trials, ZSMJ and Ruofeng became the last two players on the FTD roster, and Ruofeng moved to Hangzhou to begin professional training.

FTD had probably been assembled from five carry players in the hope of maximizing raw strength, and the trouble started the moment training began, because none of the five was willing to play support. Ruofeng was the one assigned to it. He did not take it well. He had never played the position and was not good at it, but a team needs a division of labor, so he set about learning the Demon Witch, the support hero known in English as Lion. He asked a lot of people for advice and practiced that one hero and nothing else, every day. A month later he still was not good enough. He was crushed, felt he was holding the team back, left a letter for his teammates and walked away.

Being blocked twice on the road to a professional career hit him hard, but he still did not stop training, and at WCG in 2010 an opening finally came. TOT's player chuan held foreign citizenship and could not compete in the China region of WCG, so the team pulled Ruofeng in at short notice to take over chuan's carry position. He trained intensively in Suzhou for fifteen days, drilling against famous professionals such as 430 and KSSSS. Chances came in the tournament itself, but the team still lost to NV, LGD and Ehome, all of them already established, and Ruofeng found himself back where he started.

Losing WCG 2010 was the point at which he began to understand things. On one side he had gone two years without earning money and without going to school, which left him feeling guilty toward his family. On the other side he was disappointed in his own Dota, and had begun to suspect he was not suited to the road he had taken. So he promised his mother he would go back to school, hoping it would give his parents some comfort. He picked up his books again, crammed, got into a three year college program at Hunan Media University, and began a different sort of life.

## Open minded parents and an unfinished promise

Once his mother had seen how seriously he treated his esports dream, she made a promise: if he wanted to play professionally while he was at college, his parents would not stand in his way. In September 2010, at school, Ruofeng played League of Legends for the first time, and another journey began.

At that point Ruofeng and Ayaya were with the Dream team in Changsha, and that is where he met Joker, now the coach at WE. We entered the TGA tournament run by Tencent, training online every day, and within a month we were inside the top thirty on the Chinese server and had beaten every team we faced online. I still remember the night of that final match. Everyone in our dormitory was watching him play, and he and his teammates kept talking things through over voice chat. By the time they won it was nearly dawn, and Ruofeng let out a roar that the whole building probably heard. It felt wonderful to win like that. Unfortunately, at the offline TGA final, they lost to Ehome, at the time the only professional League of Legends team in the country. Even so, the run got them noticed.

## An invitation from the esports powerhouse WE

The way Ruofeng and his teammates played convinced WE's club managers Pei Le, known as KING, and Zhou Hao, known as ZAX, that they were worth backing. After a good deal of discussion, Ruofeng and his teammates made up their minds to travel to Shanghai and join Team WE, the world famous esports club, as professional League of Legends players.

Life at WE was not easy. There were still no League of Legends tournaments in China at the time, so to raise their level Ruofeng and his teammates played through 200 ping every night, from midnight until noon the next day, against foreign teams on the American servers. Because there were so few tournaments, even events with a few thousand yuan on the line drew plenty of strong teams. Those days at WE were painful and happy at the same time.

WE's stable training environment and years of management experience began to tell, and the results improved by leaps. On October 1, 2011, at the IEM world event held in Guangzhou, the WE.LOL squad beat CLG of the United States, AAA of Europe and Flash of Singapore to take their first world championship and 15,000 US dollars in prize money. In November they won the TGC grand prix and 200,000 yuan. In 2012 the wins kept coming, with titles at WGT, NGF and TGA 2012, and in August the team spent a month in Korea competing at OGN.

As captain of WE's League of Legends team, Ruofeng was also invited by Riot Games, the American company behind League of Legends, to attend its annual meeting in the United States as a representative of China's professional players. In 2012 he represented China at the Season 2 World Championship, held by Riot in Los Angeles.

## A father's love weighs more than a mountain

After losing to iG at the WCG 2011 China national final, Ruofeng was devastated. WCG carried enormous meaning for him and he badly wanted to win it. He lost, and he cried for thirty minutes. Back at the hotel he called his father, the first time he had ever picked up the phone to him unprompted. He found it strange himself. His father had spent Ruofeng's early childhood away studying, so the two of them had been apart far more than together, and there had been a period when Ruofeng resisted everything his father tried to teach him and heard all of it as nagging. Eventually his father ran out of patience and simply stopped saying anything, and for a long stretch there was almost no communication between them.

That day, Ruofeng cried on the phone and told his father he was sorry, that he felt he had let his parents down for years. What he did not expect was that his father did not blame him at all. His father encouraged him instead, and said that what he was doing now was worth doing. Years of estrangement fell away in an instant. Father and son are now good friends who call each other often to talk, and Ruofeng is happy about it.

When Yu Jingxi became the wind, he grew up. Ruofeng, Misaya.
