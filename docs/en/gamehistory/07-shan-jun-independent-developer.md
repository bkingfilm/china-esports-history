# VII The Shan Jun Enigma: A Chinese Independent Game Developer

> Originally published on the Zhihu Column (October 15, 2015). Original link: https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/20262129

**Behind the Scenes of Chinese Gaming History VII: The Shan Jun Enigma – A Chinese Independent Game Developer**
*By BBKinG*

Shan Jun’s career path is a singular one. At fifteen, he began coding real-time strategy games as an amateur; at nineteen, he won the championship at the National College Student Game Competition hosted by USTC; by twenty-three, he had become the youngest game project manager in Shanda’s history; at twenty-seven, he launched his own venture; and most recently, he released *Wind and Cloud: Fight for Glory*, a 3D fighting game for Xbox Live Arcade, garnering acclaim from both domestic and international gaming critics.

![](../../assets/images/5e91c647d3.jpg)

**"What does it mean to be an independent game developer?"**

This was the first question Shan Jun posed after returning from Tokyo Game Show 2015, upon reviewing the outline for this interview.

“Financial autonomy? Creative control? Full production independence? Is it done purely for passion rather than profit?” Shan Jun offered a wry smile, his confusion evident as he grappled with the definition of such a broad term. I had naively assumed that given his turbulent journey through China’s gaming industry, he might offer a succinct and clear answer.

Perhaps it was precisely those experiences that taught him this truth: some answers are never simple.

![](../../assets/images/4221756497.jpg)

Shan Jun was born in Shanghai on August 30, 1981. His childhood was spent in an apartment cramped to less than ten square meters. His parents divorced when he was young, and the subsequent years of emotional repression and domestic turmoil left a profound mark on his psyche. The result was a complex personality: withdrawn and introverted by nature, yet fiercely competitive and driven by an intense need for self-expression.
Shan Jun’s talent for painting unveiled a brand-new world to him.

In childhood, he drew cars and trains; in first grade, Transformers; by fourth grade, Saint Seiya. His sketches circulated among friends and classmates with ease, granting him a profound sense of accomplishment. By middle school, he began crafting his own comic series, particularly fighting manga, which he continued to produce with growing volume and depth. When he reached the first year of high school, Shan Jun was already steeped in the art of manga. Before beginning a new work, he would script approximately 50,000 words, then regularly publish hand-drawn tankōbon volumes of twenty pages each. Countless imaginative ideas wove through his creations; he even transformed many of his classmates into characters within his comics, helping his works achieve wider circulation.

On one side, Shan Jun soared through the myriad virtual worlds he conjured. On the other, his teachers and parents remained torn in a dilemma: should they support him or not? Fortunately, Shan Jun felt no confusion at that time; he once believed drawing was his future.

![](../../assets/images/fdd9ce842d.jpg)

Everything changed when his first year of high school introduced a computer programming course. Though the course lasted only half a semester, it forever altered Shan Jun’s life.

Through it, he suddenly realized that the games *Contra*, *Pac-Man*, and *Galaxian*—which he had played with his father at the Huxi Workers' Cultural Palace—were all born from those very lines of program commands displayed on the screen. This discovery thrilled him. He could finally create a living world, and fortunately, his parents had just bought him a computer, giving him ample time to study game programming.

Thus, the cardboard games he once designed out of boredom in class evolved into chess-like computer strategy games with complex attack-and-defense mechanics.
Using palette-based pixel art, he brought to life the tank designs that had long lain dormant on paper, transforming them into mobile armored vehicles equipped with rotating turrets and offering players a selection of turret types.

The world Shan Jun built continued to expand: it now included soldiers armed with rocket launchers, missile systems, multi-branch combined units, and even real-time combat.

Classmates, drawn by the buzz, flocked to his house for team matches, but he possessed only a single computer. Consequently, he programmed a split-screen mode that allowed two players to face off simultaneously on one display.

![](../../assets/images/9163b1d0e4.jpg)
During matches, someone would hold a book up to block the view from each side—the player on the left used the keyboard, while the one on the right relied on the mouse.

While this setup may seem quaint in retrospect, for a group of children in the late 1990s, the ability to both inhabit and create their own game world was a source of wonder and joy that adults could scarcely imagine.

In 1999, Shan Jun was admitted to Shanghai Jiao Tong University’s Department of Fine Arts. There, powerful computer science electives and the university library accelerated his progress in game programming.

Because Shanghai Jiao Tong University required students to wait until their sophomore year to bring a personal computer onto campus, he did not begin working on a more complex multiplayer title until he finally acquired one as a second-year student.

Shan Jun noted that this particular game was designed to be engaging even for those merely watching the spectacle. The idea stemmed from a childhood memory: he had a relative who loved playing strategy games alone at home. Even when other children visited, he would sit idly by and watch longingly, sometimes for hours on end. At the time, Shan Jun thought, “How wonderful it would be if spectators could share in the joy of the game.”
Armed with this vision, and after consulting with friends, classmates, and his older brother Shan Hui (currently a producer at Tencent’s *Daily Run*), he resolved to design a robot combat game.

![](../../assets/images/5056d31ec3.jpg)

The title boasted a unique gameplay loop. Shan Jun programmed several distinct classes of robots, each with specialized attributes: heavy gunners boasting devastating firepower, support units that captured enemies to create openings for teammates, agile infiltrators designed to flank and ambush the opposition, long-range snipers, and close-combat melee specialists.

Competitors did not control their machines in real-time via network; instead, they loaded preset algorithms onto floppy disks. This demanded the creation of complex predictive logic and execution scripts, which were then uploaded into a central match engine, allowing opposing robots to battle autonomously.

![](../../assets/images/68108a6fc8.jpg)

In essence, the contest was a showcase of artificial intelligence design, pitting algorithm against algorithm to see whose machine could most intelligently navigate unforeseen circumstances without human intervention.

Shan Jun organized an exhibition at Jiaotong University through the Computer Association, drawing a crowd of curious gaming enthusiasts.

The atmosphere on the day of the event was electric. The programs were put to the test one after another. Spectators roared with laughter as imperfect code rendered their bots adorably foolish—some spun in endless, futile circles, while others fired wildly at random, even hitting their own allies. Yet other performances were awe-inspiring, capable of analyzing complex states and eliciting gasps of amazement from the crowd.
![](../../assets/images/4176f0e9b8.jpg)

Shan Jun’s elder brother was then a student at the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). After witnessing Shan Jun’s game, he recommended that he submit it to the annual "National College Student Game Competition" hosted by USTC. It was at this tournament that Shan Jun claimed his first championship title.

However, the most profound benefit was the community he found: a circle of independent game developers.

One particular figure from Chengdu University of Electronic Science and Technology—who had taken second place in that same competition—taught him low-level system programming techniques that were entirely new to him. Most crucially, he learned how to bypass the 640K memory limit inherent to DOS. While pixel art games typically featured small images and ran within a mere one or two megabytes, this strict limitation forced many developers to compromise between stability and complexity. By overcoming this barrier, Shan Jun gained the freedom to design larger, more intricate games.

Consequently, during his junior year, Shan Jun joined a game development team at Shanghai Jiao Tong University at the invitation of an alumnus named Hu Ling; among them was another producer who would later achieve great fame: Jenova Chen.

Having held back his ultimate move for too long and honing his skills in karate, Shan Jun finally felt ready to tackle the fighting game he had always coveted. Within just two months, he built a demo featuring 3D effects using DOS. He placed combat mechanics at the core of the experience, designing complex physics calculations to support them.

### The Price of Youth

With both a team and the necessary technical foundation in place, Shan Jun’s life was finally moving in a positive direction.
Yet, for all his promise, Shan Jun’s youth proved to be both a blessing and a curse. His immaturity, coupled with a stunted ability to communicate, a paucity of collaborative experience, and an arrogance born of early triumphs, laid bare his professional vulnerabilities.

As Shan Jun would later observe, young indie developers often wear self-centeredness as a badge of honor; while it frequently fuels independent thought, it rarely translates to effective teamwork. At that juncture, Shan Jun had not yet learned to strike the delicate balance between personal vision and collective synergy. Consequently, despite assembling a roster of exceptional talent, the trio’s highly anticipated projects floundered. A chronic deficiency in communication and coordination ensured that none of their ventures met the towering expectations placed upon them.

Regrettably, this failure failed to instill a sufficient sense of vigilance within him.

The year was 2003. Shan Jun was finishing his senior year at university, while China’s online gaming industry, spearheaded by Shanda Entertainment, was experiencing an explosive boom. As a university renowned for its computer science program, Shanghai Jiao Tong University naturally became a prime hunting ground for talent. Shan Jun’s track record of winning game design competitions cleared the path smoothly, leading him directly into Shanda’s core R&D department.

After a brief stint as customer service and a two-month rotation in operations, Shan Jun was transferred to the *Legend of Mir World* project team, where he assumed responsibility for level and stage design.

He remembers that year as grueling. Though he was an avid fan of Blizzard’s *Diablo II* and held little affection for *Legend of Mir*, he threw himself into the work with relentless intensity. He understood that only through such sheer exertion could he earn the leverage to eventually design his own games.

One year later, *Legend of Mir World* achieved monumental success. At the age of twenty-three, Shan Jun realized his dream, becoming Shanda’s youngest game project manager. In this new role, he took charge of developing *Sanguo Haoxia Zhuan*, a casual tactical RPG.

![](../../assets/images/13ec8f0bda.jpg)

The defining innovation of its design lay in its asynchronous combat system, allowing players to take actions without waiting for others’ turns.
A demo was rapidly assembled, yet its balance was profoundly flawed. Shan Jun was deeply dissatisfied, but the operations team hailed it as excellent. They argued that perceived unfairness fueled players’ desire for progression; indeed, an imbalanced skill set might compel users to linger and engage more deeply with the game.

Yet Shan Jun—youthful and idealistic—digged in his heels. He refused to bend to the operational team’s commercial wisdom, clinging obstinately to his own design philosophy. He tweaked the statistics with a meticulous hand, insisting on deliberately slow growth rates.

The tension came to a head at a corporate strategy summit hosted by Chen Tianqiao. Mr. Chen, reviewing Shan Jun’s work, noted that certain design elements were quite promising and suggested they might be simplified further. Shan Jun countered immediately and without reservation: developers must hold fast to their artistic pursuits. The room fell instantly silent.

Looking back, Shan Jun feels a profound regret. He admits that his youthful arrogance blinded him not only to the golden era of understanding Chinese gaming audiences but, more crucially, caused superiors and colleagues who had once championed him to lose all faith in his judgment.

By 2006, after nearly two years of development, *Sanguo Haoxia Zhuan* was declared a failure. As the project leader, Shan Jun bore full responsibility and was dismissed by Shanda.

![](../../assets/images/3d5ec0edc8.jpg)

In those days, Shanda was widely regarded as paradise for Chinese game developers—a colossus of wealth and power with compelling products. Jiucheng, the agency just beginning to represent *World of Warcraft*, seemed pale in comparison. To be cast out from Shanda meant public disgrace within the industry; Shan Jun’s fall was nothing short of a plunge from heaven into hell.
Jolted awake from his delusion, Shan Jun began to confront his own shortcomings. Though stung by a sense of injustice, he acknowledged his deficiencies and resolved to take a pragmatic approach: studying the popularity of casual and role-playing games in China, with *MapleStory* serving as a prime example.

Shan Jun admitted that, while he clung to certain core principles, his perspective had shifted dramatically since then.

After departing Shanda, a friend’s recommendation led him to Shanghai Lingchan Company, which was just launching its game software outsourcing division. There, he took on the role of project manager.

It was during a collaborative project with Sega that Shan Jun stumbled upon a crucial development: Microsoft had proactively established an incubation center in Chengdu to prepare for the Xbox console’s entry into the Chinese market. Their intent was to curate local Chinese titles for the Xbox Live Arcade (XBLA). This presented a golden opportunity for both China’s independent gaming sector and Shan Jun personally.

The XBLA platform operated with a rigor akin to today’s Apple App Store, but even more stringent. Once a game earned approval, Microsoft championed it to a global audience. For any title of merit, this meant access to a massive player base and significant revenue potential. XBLA had effectively dismantled the traditional barriers to publishing, empowering independent developers to compete on the world stage with just a small team.

Seizing the moment, Shan Jun crafted a demo titled *Crazy Rats* using DOS—a competitive casual mini-game. Within less than three months, Microsoft finalized their partnership, not merely for the Chinese market, but for Xbox’s international expansion.

![](../../assets/images/932535ce76.jpg)

This victory bolstered his confidence immensely. He began to see viability in a strategy of “independent games + XBLA”—bypassing the domestic Chinese market entirely to target international audiences directly.
“Looking back, I really underestimated how formidable independent game development can be,” Shan Jun quipped to himself as he sat across from me.

In 2007, swayed by the persistent invitations of his old college friend Hu Ling, Shan Jun resigned from his job to launch a startup. The fledgling company consisted of merely two people: Hu Ling built the engine and tools, while Shan Jun managed the core logic, leaving art and music to be outsourced. This division of labor established their initial direction: a focus on fighting games, specifically those Shan Jun was both passionate about and adept at creating. From this came *Feng Juan Can Yun* (Wind and Cloud: Fight for Glory), a 3D fighter custom-built for the Xbox Live Arcade platform.

However, securing a spot on that platform proved far from simple. Two avenues existed. The first was to participate in Microsoft’s Dream Build Play competition; victory there offered a direct path to publication. Yet the contest was fiercely competitive, pitting them against indie developers from around the globe, with finals often narrowing down to just forty or fifty entries.

Over the next two years, Shan Jun and his team managed only one third-place finish and two second prizes. They came close, but it wasn’t enough to secure a place on XBLA.

The alternative route was to find a publisher recognized by Microsoft and submit titles through them. This path mirrored the challenges Shan Jun had faced previously in simpler terms: finding a commercial entity willing to invest in their development; once the game launched on the platform and performed well commercially, profits would be shared.

By 2007–2009, Tencent’s casual online games were generating massive profits, while Shan Jun’s console fighting game—developed specifically for European and American audiences—was clearly no longer relevant to the domestic Chinese market.
For two long years, Hu Ling and Shan Jun had struggled to secure a publisher, their savings dwindling to nothing as pressure from their families mounted. With his wife on the verge of giving birth, Hu Ling felt compelled to return to Australia to care for her. Caught between these competing demands, he proposed that he go there to earn money to support them both, allowing Shan Jun to continue working on the game unencumbered. But Shan Jun felt uneasy accepting such a burden; with polite firmness, he declined, resolving to find another path forward.

By the end of 2009, amid the booming Chinese domestic gaming industry, Shan Jun found himself at his wit’s end regarding his indie title. Doubt began to creep in: were his choices sound? Was the game heading in the right direction? Was the very concept of indie gaming viable?

![](../../assets/images/d7df344b83.jpg)

Just when he felt most lost, a turning point emerged from an unexpected quarter. Shanda 18 Fund, Shan Jun’s former employer, agreed to sponsor *Wind and Cloud: Fight for Glory* with a tens-of-millions investment and granted him three years to develop the product—a remarkably favorable deal.

With that backing, Shan Jun immediately assembled a team of over a dozen people. Leveraging Shanda’s endorsement, he almost instantaneously secured a partnership with a foreign game publisher and finalized a collaboration with XBLA. Overnight, what had taken two years of fruitless effort was accomplished in the blink of an eye.

Funding for development? Secured. Access to the Xbox platform? Granted.

Was it a happy ending?

In truth, I would love to end the article here, to tell everyone that Shan Jun and his indie game went on to live happily ever after. But that would be a lie.
But reality is invariably cruel. In the two years that followed, two seismic shifts occurred.

First, while the initial month of *Wind and Cloud: Fight for Glory*’s release saw eight thousand copies sold to warm acclaim from players both at home and abroad—such that some fans still haunt its Tieba forums today—the momentum shattered at a critical juncture when expansion was paramount. The foreign distributor fell out with Microsoft; consequently, all of their titles were purged from the platform, dragging *Wind and Cloud: Fight for Glory* down with them and pulling it from sale.

Second, Shanda’s 18th Fund ceased operations abruptly. With Shan Jun’s team having completed merely half of their second project, they found themselves stranded in a precarious gap: no incoming revenue, effectively running out of food and firewood.
By 2013, after weathering relentless storms and struggling to keep their heads above water for years, Shan Jun’s endurance finally snapped. Disheartened and disillusioned, he surrendered his dream of creating independent games, pivoting instead to commercial development. Once again, he embarked on the grueling journey of traveling with demo discs in hand, seeking sponsorship. But he was no longer fighting alone; he now bore responsibility for over twenty brothers who had stood by him through thick and thin, each of them weighed down by their own pressing needs for livelihood and family.

"Transitioning from entrepreneurship—making games *I* enjoyed—to assuming responsibility for others and creating games *they* like," Shan Jun reflected, "was a profound shift in my life. It carries no inherent moral weight; it is neither good nor bad, right nor wrong. I have not abandoned the desire to make the games I want to make. I have merely become more mature and less extreme. Thus, when I revisit the questions 'What is an indie game? What is an indie developer?' from this new vantage point, my understanding has grown both more complex and more grounded."

— Shan Jun, Chinese Indie Game Developer
