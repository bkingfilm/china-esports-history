# 8. Huang Yimeng, the CEO, is No Longer One of the Angry Youths

> Originally published on Zhihu Column (2016-08-08). Original link: https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/21668259

**Behind the Scenes of Chinese Gaming History: Part 8**
**Huang Yimeng, the CEO, is No Longer One of the Angry Youths**

By BBKinG

Following our interview, I asked Huang Yimeng, "Dash, regarding these stories of the angry youths, may I truly write them down?"

Huang Yimeng was born in Changning District, Shanghai, on March 18, 1982.

At that moment, it was still twenty years until he founded VeryCD and introduced eMule (Electric Donkey) to China.
It was still twenty-one years until he was expelled from university by administrative decree.

**Angry Youth: The Huang Yimeng Story**

Huang’s mother worked as a laboratory technician, while his father was a university physical education instructor who later rose to become the head of the department. This dual heritage profoundly shaped Huang Yimeng in two key ways.

The first was affluence. His parents possessed open minds, which allowed him to encounter video game consoles, computers, and the internet at a tender age. The second was discipline; from childhood, his father compelled him to undergo rigorous physical training within the sports system.

Huang recalls that his father was an exceptionally cheerful man who played *Gradius* and *Contra* with him during those early years. Yet, when it came to athletics, he was stern and demanding.

For instance, while teaching him to swim, his father made him repeat a single movement all day long. And because Huang Yimeng was short for his age in the second grade of elementary school, his father thrust him onto the basketball team. While other children went out to play after school each day, he was required to remain on campus, drilling shots until dusk.
Before the NBA and *Slam Dunk* captured the public imagination, these training sessions were excruciatingly tedious for Huang Yimeng. The monotony fostered a quiet resistance within him against this rigid educational approach.

**The Awakening of Self-Identity**

Huang recalls drifting aimlessly through life prior to his third year of middle school. With grades hovering around the mediocre mark, he felt ordinary, unsure of his passions or his future.

That changed one day when an experimental group from East China Normal University visited the school to administer an intelligence test. Finding the questions manageable, Huang finished quickly and submitted his paper. Later, the teacher summoned him for a private conversation. “You are remarkably bright,” she told him. “If you applied that same energy to your studies, your grades would reflect it.”

It was then that Huang learned he had tied for the highest score in the class with another student who was already renowned as an excellent scholar.

From that moment, a realization took root: perhaps he was not like everyone else.

He looked back on his childhood experiences—programming with a learning machine, experimenting with games on his father’s laptop in the early 1990s, and later encountering Pascal, a structured programming language, at the Changning District Young Scientists Station during middle school. These encounters had subtly steered him toward a specific path.

Huang described it as a sudden moment of clarity. Coinciding with the rise of the internet in China, he discovered tech luminaries like Bill Gates and learned that they had dropped out of school before founding their empires. This knowledge bolstered his resolve to pursue programming, despite his growing disdain for formal schooling. He began teaching himself various technologies online, charting his own course.
Noting that his son was not merely indulging in gaming addiction, but actively extracting knowledge from the machine, his father chose to let it be. Yet the rigid confines of high school left Huang Yimeng profoundly bewildered. Why must he toil over subjects that appeared utterly devoid of utility? His disdain for formal education deepened with every passing day.

Huang’s performance on the 2000 National College Entrance Examination was far from impressive. In a twist of irony that brought both laughter and tears, the physical training that had once tormented him became his salvation. Leveraging his status as a Class II National Athlete, he secured admission to Shanghai University’s Business Administration program through special sports enrollment quotas.

It was only then that the truth unveiled itself: this was the grand chess game his father had meticulously orchestrated over eighteen years. Huang remarked on this realization with a wry, half-joking tone.

Once enrolled in university, he discovered the intoxicating luxury of absolute freedom; no eyes were upon him to constrain his actions. This liberty allowed his technical prowess to soar. During his freshman year, Huang Yimeng generated substantial income by developing websites and even coded his own forum software using PHP. The arrogance of youth took hold: looking across the nascent landscape of the internet, he believed there was nothing he could not build.

**The Birth of VeryCD**

In 2001, Huang’s family became part of a privileged few in Shanghai to install broadband internet, granting them high-speed access to digital materials—a convenience largely denied to the public at large, yet desperately desired.

Capitalizing on this gap, he conceived a novel commercial model: a directory website aggregating everything users sought. Clients would select their desired content, generate an order, and Huang would burn the files onto physical discs and ship them to them. He noted that his initial concept was straightforward—to sell CDs via this service—which gave rise to the name “VeryCD.”
Yet, while Huang was immersed in his online material collection for CD sales, a technological shift upended his plans: the rise of peer-to-peer file sharing. He realized that downloading files directly was far more efficient than burning physical discs.

Accordingly, Huang pivoted his strategy, launching a forum dedicated to VeryCD. There, he listed directories of the content he had gathered and configured his home computer to act as an FTP server, enabling users to freely exchange resources through the forum’s interface.

Soon, word of mouth transformed VeryCD into Shanghai University’s most popular digital hub. The platform’s activity surged during the SARS outbreak of 2003; with schools imposing lockdowns and students across China confined to their homes, the forum became a vital lifeline for boredom-stricken youth, growing increasingly vibrant by the day.

Just as Huang Yimeng reveled in his website’s rising popularity, a shocking event erupted on campus that would grip the entire nation:

**The Shanghai University Hidden Camera Incident**

Those who accessed the internet from school networks during that era likely still recall the incident vividly. Several students had pointed DV camcorders toward an adjacent teaching building and inadvertently captured footage of a couple engaging in sexual intercourse within a classroom. The shot was taken from a considerable distance, leaving their features unidentifiable. Once this video hit the internet, it ignited a sensation.

In contemporary times, such scandals might seem commonplace, but in 2003, during the infancy of China’s web culture, the footage triggered astonishing chain reactions. It was uploaded and downloaded via FTP countless times across the country, spreading like wildfire.

Among the venues that hosted this file were several internal university FTP servers and Huang Yimeng’s VeryCD forum. Sensing a crisis, the university launched a crackdown, sealing off information and handling the matter in strict secrecy, hoping to erase it from public memory. Under intense pressure from various quarters, all campus FTP servers were ordered to be wiped clean. Yet, no matter how much pressure was applied, Huang Yimeng refused to comply.
Huang later explained that he had been nothing more than an angry young man at the time. Although the voyeuristic content had no bearing on him—nor did he upload it himself—he vehemently opposed the university’s strategy of concealing the incident and forcibly suppressing it through administrative decree. He believed such tactics had no place in the nascent internet landscape.

It was a lesson learned too soon: Huang Yimeng paid the price for his naivety.

He was summoned one day by the local police station affiliated with the university. The officers accused him of disseminating obscene materials, slapped him with a bail pending trial document on charges of insult, and confiscated his home computer.

The repercussions extended further still: he was expelled alongside several students who had filmed the videos.

The incident plunged Huang’s parents into distress. As lifelong public servants, they found such an "accident" utterly unfathomable. Yet Huang himself seemed unbothered, even quietly pleased.

He described being ordered to withdraw as an unexpected gift. He had long harbored the desire to drop out, but his parents would never have consented to a voluntary resignation. In this case, however, they had no choice.

On the night of his withdrawal, Huang Yimeng was electrified with excitement. The prospect of waking up the next day to do whatever he wished kept him awake in anticipation.

The following morning, he set about transforming the VeryCD forum into a fully independent website.
Events unfolded with accelerating speed. While site traffic surged steadily, its explosive growth triggered a relentless cycle of suspensions by various virtual hosting providers. With no alternative, Huang secured his own server. Lacking any revenue and reliant solely on the 2,000 yuan donated by forum users—a sum insufficient for purchase—he was forced to rent one instead.

To generate some income, Huang joined an ad network, but this proved inadequate; he had no choice but to seek employment. In 2003, while browsing [http://PHPcn.net](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//PHPcn.net), a site he frequented, Huang landed a web programmer position through a contact at the website. The monthly salary of 4,000 yuan exceeded what his parents earned, bringing a measure of relief to his family.

As traffic and advertising revenue swelled, Huang diligently used every opportunity to lease additional servers as soon as he could afford them. Consequently, the number of servers supporting VeryCD quickly expanded from one to ten. Although profits remained marginal, this rapid expansion allowed Huang Yimeng to glimpse a viable path forward.

Four months later, Huang resigned and partnered with Dai Yunjie and four others to rent a cramped apartment on Tianshan Road—barely large enough to accommodate four desks—thereby officially launching their startup.
Dai Yunjie, too, is a character worth noting. He and Huang Yimeng had been high school classmates at Fudan High School. Though their families lived in close proximity, they had never crossed paths—Dai was the archetypal top student, while Huang was notoriously ill-suited to academics, though his aptitude with computers was already evident.

Their paths finally converged in the summer of their graduation, when Dai visited Huang’s home to learn web development. It was then that they discovered a shared passion for the new and unfamiliar. When Dai later enrolled at Shanghai University, he and Huang began working on VeryCD together, forging a partnership of friendship and colleagueship that endures to this day.

By the summer of 2004, VeryCD’s servers were under considerable strain. Although the site relied on peer-to-peer technology—meaning data did not flow directly through their own infrastructure—the volume of traffic during evening peak hours continued to surge. Unable to afford additional hardware, they had to devise alternative methods for load balancing.

Cloud computing was still a distant concept; automatic load distribution programs did not yet exist, forcing them to manage everything by hand. Huang recalls those nights with vivid clarity: whenever the load grew heavy, he and Dai would perform manual micro-adjustments on the servers, distributing traffic across different machines to ensure service remained accessible. Once the server tweaks were complete, they would unwind by playing *Warcraft III*, a game that likewise demanded precise micro-management. This hands-on experience proved invaluable, giving XD Network the deep understanding of server load issues it would later need when transitioning into game development.

In 2005, Cai Wensheng invested one million RMB into VeryCD. For the first time, Huang Yimeng had the liquid capital to purchase servers, gradually replacing their rented equipment. He also hired a software team dedicated to developing the Electric Donkey client (eMule).

It is worth clarifying the origin of the name “Electric Donkey.” Huang Yimeng explained that they had not chosen the name themselves; it was coined by netizens. Later, when critics accused them of conflating mules with donkeys, he felt a profound sense of grievance.

Literally translated, eMule means “Electric Mule.” The original eMule was an open-source download client imported from abroad, serving as the foundation for Huang Yimeng’s Electric Donkey. However, this original eMule was itself built upon a commercial software called eDonkey2000. Since “eDonkey” literally translates to “Electric Donkey,” the developers of the open-source version adopted the mule—a hybrid superior to both donkeys and horses—as a symbol of their advantage over eDonkey2000. Consequently, netizens effectively redirected the moniker back to “Electric Donkey,” creating a somewhat murky semantic situation.

From 2005 to 2008, while VeryCD continued to expand, its operational costs remained prohibitively high. Reliant primarily on advertising revenue—which was frequently plagued by unpaid fees—the company could barely break even.

What weighed most heavily on Huang Yimeng’s mind, however, was the rapidly shifting market landscape.

First, public awareness of copyright law grew, and regulatory policies tightened. The environment evolved from an early era of lax enforcement to active copyright disputes, and eventually to strict state emphasis on film copyrights. As a result, shareable resources dwindled, marking a significant trend.

Second, competitors such as Xunlei and online video-on-demand platforms began to rise. These companies secured substantial investment and possessed superior technology, causing Electric Donkey’s user growth to stall.
Most critically, Huang Yimeng believed his strategic vision had been insufficient during VeryCD’s nascent stages. Unclear on the website’s future trajectory, he was overly idealistic, neglected to consider return on investment, and lacked corporate management experience regarding equity structure. This oversight rendered him unable to pivot when he eventually sought to do so.

Between 2008 and 2009, Huang explored numerous avenues for transformation. He considered manufacturing TV set-top boxes and contemplated launching a video website, yet neither path offered a particularly viable profit model at the time.

It was not until browser games ascended to prominence that VeryCD, acting as a channel partner, began co-publishing titles such as *Wulin Hero*, *Sanguo Fengyun*, and *Business Tycoon*. Surprisingly, this venture generated monthly revenue of one million yuan for the company.

Huang realized that game publishing yielded higher and more stable returns than advertising. Moreover, having played games since childhood and harboring a deep passion for them, he had obsessively grappled with *Command & Conquer* and *Quake II* on his home PC. As esports gained popularity, he excelled at *CS*, *WAR3*, and *StarCraft*, even participating in competitive tournaments. His home housed gaming consoles from every era, including GameBoy, NDS, PlayStation, and XBOX.
From his vantage point, the existing games were crude yet undeniably lucrative. It struck him as a logical pursuit: why not craft his own title and manage its operation independently?

In 2009, determined to sever ties with the equity constraints of his previous venture, Huang made the grim decision to sell his URL aggregation platform, [http://7999.com](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//7999.com)—which was still generating healthy profits—at the price of 8 million yuan. The net proceeds, however, amounted to only 4 million. He immediately allocated 1 million toward acquiring the domain [http://XD.com](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//XD.com) and another 500,000 to renovate a new office space. The remaining 2.5 million was earmarked for recruiting a large team dedicated to independent game development.

Self-development carried significant risk; projects were a roll of the dice, yielding both triumphs and failures. His earliest attempt, an SNS-based mini-game, ended in disaster, leading to the dissolution of the entire project team.

By 2010, when his first independently developed title, *Tiandi Yingxiong*, finally launched, the company’s finances were dangerously lean. Operating from two floors on Tianyaoqiao Road in Shanghai with a staff exceeding one hundred, this was nothing short of a monumental gamble.

Fortunately, it paid off. Shortly after launch, *Tiandi Yingxiong* generated 30 million yuan in revenue, significantly bolstering Huang Yimeng’s conviction to continue his pursuit of the gaming industry. With the subsequent release of *Shenxian Dao*, the entire landscape of the gaming channel was overturned.

This transformation reached back to 2005, when Cai Wensheng hosted a webmaster conference in Xiamen. That event cast a long shadow over the future of China’s internet industry, serving as a critical nexus where many pivotal figures first crossed paths.
It was at this conference that Huang Yimeng crossed paths with Cai Wensheng, as well as A Fei, the founder of [http://ChinaZ.com](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//ChinaZ.com), who would later helm Feiyu Technology, the studio behind *Shenxian Dao*.

In 2010, buoyed by the success of *Tiandi Yingxiong*, A Fei sent Huang a demo of his new game. Impressed by its quality, Huang sought to secure publishing rights as an agent. However, bidding wars with other gaming companies drove the advance payment up to four million yuan.

Four million yuan might seem modest today, but in those days, it was an exorbitant sum. Not long before, Huang had been able to independently develop multiple titles on a mere 2.5 million yuan. Although XinDong did not ultimately submit the highest bid, *Shenxian Dao* landed with them; A Fei was convinced by XinDong’s track record with *Tiandi Yingxiong* and valued the strategic advice they offered for the game.

At the time, XinDong had no intention of specializing in publishing; it was primarily a development house with a lean operations team. Consequently, Huang assigned the project management of *Shenxian Dao* to Liu Yifeng, a newly hired former professional *Warcraft III* player who had just retired from competitive play. Having played esports himself, Huang believed that anyone capable of competing at a professional level possessed exceptional intellect.

Huang recalls that *Shenxian Dao*’s launch was seamless, with monthly revenues surpassing 100 million yuan in less than six months. He attributes this success to three key factors.
First, the game boasted relatively high-quality graphics. As a horizontally scrolling title, it allowed players to see numerous characters on screen simultaneously—a rarity among web games at that time.

Then there was the abundance of servers left over from his tenure at VeryCD, coupled with years of accumulated server operations experience. This enabled Xiandong to maintain operational costs significantly lower than its competitors and to launch new servers within seconds—an industry first. By contrast, other operators required at least three to seven days for the same task.

Furthermore, due to these low costs, Xiandong could offer channel partners—including certain celebrities—a revenue share exceeding 70%, a proposition that other game operators simply could not match.

Thus, fueled by this combination of internal and external factors, *Shenxian Dao* disrupted the prevailing channel operation strategies of the era and achieved remarkable success.

Between 2011 and 2012, Huang Yimeng’s son and daughter were born in succession. In 2012, on his thirtieth birthday, he gifted himself a Lamborghini.

Huang recalled that on the day his son was born, he drove home along an elevated highway; the setting sun from the opposite lane bathed his face in light, and he felt an indescribable sense of joy.

Career success, familial happiness, and the blessing of both a son and a daughter—this was the happiest period of Huang Yimeng’s life. However, the narrative did not end there; what followed became the true focus of the story.

Soon after, a web game plagiarizing *Shenxian Dao* emerged. Its monetization design was incredibly aggressive, generating even higher revenue than its original inspiration. Legally, however, XD Network had no recourse against this infringement.

For channel partners, such nuances were irrelevant. Their strategy was simple: promote whichever game turned a profit most rapidly and efficiently.
This ultimatum compelled *Shenxian Dao* to overhaul its system and seek new revenue streams, leaving XD Network with no choice but to comply. Although the company managed to swiftly reclaim its market share, this strategy was tantamount to slaughtering the goose that laid the golden eggs; it severely undermined the numerical balance of the game in its later stages.

Huang lamented his failure to reflect more deeply on the matter. Following *Shenxian Dao*'s monumental success, his confidence swelled to arrogance; he came to believe that merely transplanting past experience into new projects would guarantee future triumphs.

Consequently, XD Network acquired a slate of new titles. While some yielded respectable data, none could match the legendary heights of *Shenxian Dao*. Huang Yimeng began to ponder why domestic games were steadily becoming less engaging.

He admitted that after *Shenxian Dao*, he lost interest in new releases, finding them all indistinguishable and difficult to get into. Even titles topping the bestseller charts across various download channels failed to hold his attention. At the time, however, he did not view this as a critical issue; he dismissed it merely as aesthetic fatigue from playing too many games.

But one platform sparked new thoughts within him: Steam.

Huang noted, "Have you noticed how strange and unconventional many games on Steam are? Their creativity knows no bounds, offering moments of genuine surprise with every playthrough. Beyond a single purchase, they rarely employ other monetization tactics. Why aren't domestic games like this?"

By 2013, the golden age of browser games had drawn to a close. Major game companies rushed to pivot toward mobile gaming, and Xindong was no exception, diving headfirst into another round of fierce competition.
Soon, Huang Yimeng realized that the same old problem had resurfaced: the games crowning the best-seller charts across various platforms were indistinguishable from one another, rendering them utterly unenjoyable to him. He recalled that incident where they had fought for channel access against knock-off titles. Could the issue lie in their cooperation model?

Huang traced this clue back to its source, identifying what he termed the "original sin" of cooperative operations. At the time, XD Network leveraged the lucrative revenue-sharing structure of *Shenxian Dao* to incentivize its channel partners, reaping enormous profits for both developers and distributors alike. This success triggered a wave of industry-wide imitation.

Over the subsequent years, these channels grew into colossi, transforming the gaming industry into a mere "traffic monetization sector" rife with opaque practices. Huang explained that the logic was straightforward but flawed: channel capacity is limited. To secure profits and meet performance targets, editors shunned Steam titles—innovative games, indies, or premium one-off purchases—in favor of designs with mature numerical systems and higher per-user spending potential.

Furthermore, the competition among channels was ferocious. In their bid to attract users, they resorted to vicious tactics such as guild discounts and subsidized top-ups, severely undermining both the numerical balance of games and fair play. This disorderly race to the bottom drove operators to act recklessly. To climb revenue-based leaderboards and secure channel recommendations, they spent heavily to manipulate rankings, aggressively pushed monetization hooks, and did everything in their power to convince channels that their games were profit machines.

Consequently, the titles dominating major Chinese charts have become increasingly homogenized and exorbitantly expensive to play.
Huang explained that the more lucrative a game proved, the larger its share of joint-operation revenue, and consequently, the greater the promotional resources channels would pour into it. This dynamic represented both the strength and the flaw of the co-publishing model: driven by greed, channels would perpetually favor high-spending titles over enjoyable ones, as long as promotion continued. This vicious cycle only deepened with time.

Yet, could one really ask these platforms to abandon co-publishing, surrender their revenue shares, and ignore profit entirely—to spit out the meat already in their jaws?

Huang Yimeng’s response was simple: “If so, let us build one ourselves.”

Thus, [TapTap](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//TapTap.com) was born.

In designing the platform, Huang drew parallels to VeryCD of old. While that earlier service had helped users locate information they sought as a sharing hub, it did nothing to support content creators financially; indeed, it often harmed them.

The new platform, therefore, had to serve two masters: helping players discover games they loved, and enabling developers to earn a living.

To counteract human greed, the design relied on decentralization and minimal human intervention.

Rankings were stripped of financial metrics, editorial curation was abolished, co-publishing was discarded, and recommendations rested solely on reviews from friends and the community.

Crucially, the platform itself had to become profitable. Huang Yimeng was no longer the naive youth once dismissed as “too young, too foolish”; he knew that sustainability was paramount. His strategy was clear: once the user base reached a critical mass, TapTap would monetize through game advertisements.

He argued that many modern game companies possessed a keen desire to advertise. TapTap’s users were deeply passionate about gaming, and the current market landscape was far more fertile than it had been when VeryCD launched a decade prior. As long as ads were clearly labeled, users would download games that truly delivered enjoyment.

Once the disruptor that reshaped the gaming landscape through joint operations, Xindong now seeks to dismantle that very model and shake up the industry once more.

Huang recounted how the first hundred days of TapTap brought him an outpouring of praise. The reception from both players and publishers affirmed his confidence in the model, reigniting the sense of accomplishment he had felt during his VeryCD era. Yet his perspective has sharpened; looking further ahead, he aspires to transform TapTap into the "Steam for mobile," while placing even greater emphasis on cultivating community.

After the interview concluded, I turned to Huang Yimeng and asked, "Dash, can I truly write about this—the story of the angry youth?"

"I am no longer an angry youth," he replied.
