# V Tianlang’s Dream of Industrializing Cosplay

> Originally published on the Zhihu Column (2014-10-25). Original link: https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/19863755

**Behind the Scenes of Chinese Gaming History, Part 5**

### Tianlang’s Dream of Industrializing Cosplay

**By BBKinG**

Born on March 26, 1986, in Hongkou District, Shanghai, Jiang Hui embodies the quintessential Aries spirit: dynamic, fiercely competitive, and fiery. While many know him better by his professional moniker, "Tianlang," he remains a towering figure within China’s cosplay community.

Behind the scenes, Tianlang is the driving force behind Langsheng Culture and Art Planning Co., Ltd., an enterprise renowned in its field. The company serves as the exclusive manufacturer of cosplay props and the primary event execution partner for tech giants such as Tencent and NetEase, leaving its mark at high-profile events like the annual League of Legends anniversary celebration.

**What Is Cosplay?**

"Cosplay" is a portmanteau of the English words *costume* and *play*. It refers to the art of dressing in costumes, accessories, props, and makeup to portray characters from anime or video games. Though it originated in the United States and flourished in Japan, the definition of cosplay has broadened significantly today. Its subjects range from fictional characters to real-life figures, and even specific archetypes of individuals. Enthusiasts generally refer to themselves as cosplayers.

Tianlang entered the industry as a performer before branching out to host events. From 2005 to the present day, he has served as the master of ceremonies for the ChinaJoy Cosplay National Carnival, witnessing firsthand the promotional cycles of major titles like *World of Warcraft* and *League of Legends* during Jiucheng’s tenure.

Today, Tianlang’s company is based in Hangzhou. He has leased two villas on the property: one dedicated to storing cosplay costumes and equipment, and the other serving as both his office and residence.
He explained that his choice of Hangzhou was driven not only by its lower cost of living compared to Shanghai, but also by the city’s abundant artistic resources.

Cosplay demands substantial graphic design work, while prop creation requires high standards for sculpting techniques and specialized equipment—needs that are well served by the China Academy of Art in Hangzhou. The city is rich in silk and various fabrics, essential materials for costume production, while the garment-making expertise required during manufacturing can be found among the specialized professionals in the clothing department at Zhejiang Sci-Tech University. Furthermore, countless small workshops proliferate to meet the demand for accessories of all sizes.

Additionally, the makeup and performance disciplines offered by Zhejiang University of Media and Communications provide unique conditions that foster the industrialization of cosplay.

In recent years, Tianlang and his team have enjoyed a considerably higher standard of living. Tencent Game, their partner for many years, affords them ample time and creative freedom to fulfill commissions, along with substantial financial support.

However, more than ten years ago, Tianlang’s life was far from easy.

The divorce of his parents fueled his rage, forging a highly rebellious personality. He moved out during his second year of high school and began working part-time at a young age. To find employment, he persistently pleaded with recruitment agencies, but they turned him away due to his youth. Eventually, he settled for a position as a data entry clerk, typing for hours each day until the ink stained all ten fingers black.

Tianlang had developed an interest in cosplay by this stage, yet balancing part-time work and school left him no opportunity to participate. He explained that his reasons for loving cosplay were complex, encompassing a sense of identity, achievement, the search for like-minded communities, and an escape from reality.
When SARS erupted in 2003, the streets were fraught with peril, prompting Tianlang’s teacher to advise him to return home. With his nomadic days behind him, he finally found the opportunity to pursue cosplay on his own terms. He joined a troupe and, fortuitously, was cast as the lead male in his debut *Gintama* cosplay.

Yet cosplay is an expensive pursuit. In those early days, specialized prop-makers and costume artisans were few and far between, leaving enthusiasts to source materials and craft every piece by hand. The process was arduous, costly, and exacting; consequently, he took on additional part-time work to sustain his passion. He also trained as a cosplay event host, a role that typically commanded higher wages.

By chance in 2004, Tianlang learned of campus promotion activities for the ChinaJoy Cosplay National Carnival in Shanghai. He volunteered for the task. His outstanding performance caught the eye of OME Electronic Games, who invited him to host their booth at that year’s ChinaJoy. This connection introduced him to the head of Hanwei Exhibition Co., the organizer of the event, and marked the beginning of his work on the ChinaJoy Cosplay National Carnival.

It was a convergence of timing, place, and people—pure serendipity.
By 2004, Shanda’s IPO had ignited an era of explosive growth for the Chinese gaming industry. This surge spurred a demand to promote a wide array of anime and game titles, including Jiucheng’s World of Warcraft, Giant Interactive’s Zhengtu, Snail Games’ Mabinogi, and Shanda’s Aion. Consequently, the market became inundated with numerous anime and gaming products eager for promotional campaigns.
Tianlang rode the crest of this wave. His Cosplay club, Mirror People, became a long-term supplier for these companies. At its zenith, his firm handled nearly every Cosplay commission in Hall 1 of ChinaJoy. It had previously gained renown for producing a highly celebrated set of Gold Saints costumes titled "Golden Twelve Palaces."

During those years, Tianlang evolved from merely hosting Cosplay events to orchestrating their execution, accumulating substantial experience and professional connections that laid the groundwork for his future entrepreneurial ventures.

In 2008, the controversy surrounding the rights transfer of *World of Warcraft* in China sent shockwaves through the industry.

With a new administration came a new regime; the incoming distributor rejected the existing supplier network, resulting in fewer projects being assigned to Tianlang. He even considered abandoning the field entirely.

Just then, the national four-trillion-yuan stimulus package, launched in response to the 2008 global financial crisis, pulled Tianlang back into Cosplay.

A portion of this funding was directed at supporting the automotive industry, causing the national auto show market to boom. Coupled with the release of the live-action *Transformers* film, a business opportunity arose when the renowned American brand Chevrolet sought a Bumblebee Autobot for a nationwide promotional tour. This chance meeting once again put food on Tianlang's table.

The contract was secured, but Tianlang suffered in silence. The task was tedious: he merely had to take a pre-fabricated Bumblebee suit on a nationwide tour. The itinerary and content were rigidly identical, leaving no room for improvisation.

Tianlang explained that in 2010, they took up *League of Legends*—then an obscure title—purely by accident while desperate to escape such mind-numbing monotony.
In March 2010, a phone call from Poppy at Tencent Games would set in motion events that altered the course of Tianlang’s future.

Poppy explained that for their upcoming game launch, they needed eighteen to twenty cosplay models.

The request came as a shock. Previously, when gaming studios sought cosplayers, they typically limited their commissions to four or five characters, categorized by class roles—warriors, rogues, hunters, healers, and so on. Even during the height of *World of Warcraft* expansions, no more than eight characters were ever commissioned. Tianlang had never seen Tencent approach a project with such scale.

He later reflected that, had this offer arrived before 2008, he likely would have dismissed it as unreliable. But the cosplay circuit had become so tedious that he accepted without hesitation. From that moment on, his life entered an entirely new chapter.

Before founding his own company in 2009, Tianlang took stock of the industry and faced a choice: either pursue fame as a solo cosplayer and monetize his public profile, or operate as a service provider for clients such as anime and game publishers.

While he acknowledged that some cosplayers managed to earn between 300,000 and 500,000 RMB annually through events and the sale of merchandise like photo prints, the income was erratic. He felt the time was not yet ripe for such a path, believing one must first secure survival before considering growth. Thus, he firmly resolved to establish a company and take the second route.

“Even if my clients treat me with a thousand forms of torment, I shall greet them as my first love.”
In 2010, to help the team survive, Tianlang accepted commissions from clients other than Tencent alongside his existing contracts. For instance, he crafted cosplay props for Fan Bingbing in *King of Kings III* and fulfilled orders for ChinaJoy on behalf of Changyou.

It was not until September 2011 that Tianlang suddenly realized *League of Legends* had truly gained popularity; consequently, the orders and event requirements from Tencent Games began to multiply relentlessly. Even if he poured his entire company’s resources into these projects, they still could not keep pace.

The Promotion of League of Legends

Initially, Tianlang employed stage plays that depicted opposing factions to narrate the game’s background lore—a marketing tactic frequently used since the era of *World of Warcraft*. Everyone remembers the phrase: "For the Horde!" It was a highly successful case study in marketing.

This led to the stage play on September 22, 2012, for *League of Legends’* first anniversary, where every step taken by the cosplayer portraying Prince elicited shouts of "Demacia," ultimately sparking the phenomenon-level popularity of the slogan "Demacia!" across China.

However, times had changed, and player expectations had shifted. Tianlang discovered that during the early stages of promotion, many new players cared little for the backstory. In fact, for many new games, the lore was often a mishmash of mythological and historical figures blended into narratives by opposing factions in stage plays. The audience’s reaction was far less intense than it would be for displays of exaggerated physical features or for the rapid-fire appearance of heroes.

What does "spamming heroes" mean?

It refers to the practice of numerous cosplayers taking the stage one by one to strike a pose and recite their iconic game lines. For example: "Your sword is my sword!" Such moments were met with thunderous applause from the audience.

This situation caused Tianlang considerable distress.
By then, Tianlang possessed ample experience, manpower, and resources to refine every aspect of production—from direction and scriptwriting to stage design, but especially prop-making. He imposed industrial-grade polishing standards on his work, willing to seclude himself for three months, laboring eighteen hours a day with nothing but the crafting of props in mind.

Such dedication yielded the "Future Warrior" cosplay, which garnered widespread acclaim during *League of Legends*’ second anniversary and even drew congratulatory messages from overseas peers. Yet Tianlang was not content to merely showcase heroes on stage through costume plays; he noted that this very limitation had once driven his team into a bottleneck.

Fortunately, as the game’s popularity expanded, its competitive spirit began to win public acceptance. With the introduction of hero backstories and the real-world struggles of esports athletes, the Tianlang team felt emboldened to reintegrate those competitive elements into their performances. During the third-anniversary event, “Heart Against Korea,” audiences were able to relive the thrill of the game through the lens of cosplay.

Tianlang expressed his hope that one day, cosplay would resonate more deeply with players, encouraging them to sit down and share stories about the heroes they love, their personal experiences in the game, epic sessions with friends, and hilarious misplays—because this is our game, and it mirrors our lives.

So, where should cosplay go from here? Tianlang had pondered this extensively. In the United States, Marvel Comics had cultivated mature intellectual property through years of development, achieving tremendous success by adapting these IPs into live-action films that featured anime-inspired aesthetics. Since Cosplay also originated from such virtual media, could it eventually return to become the cradle of live-action cinema? Or perhaps become an integral part of it?

Of course, this was a distant road. For Tianlang, the immediate headache lay in the industry’s lack of understanding and its disorderly competition.
He describes this predicament as reminiscent of the esports landscape a few years past: first, there was a profound lack of understanding and support from their families, who viewed cosplay not as a viable profession but as a frivolous hobby. Second, the industry lacked established protocols. If one were to cultivate a talented cosplayer or team, competitors would swiftly attempt to poach them with lucrative offers, disregarding breach-of-contract penalties and forcing them away, leaving the original cultivators with little recourse. Unless this disorder is resolved, no one can develop in peace, let alone expand.

He believes that perhaps the industry remains too small in scale, and the timing is simply not yet ripe.

Consequently, Tianlang has devoted greater energy to refining every stage of the cosplay production process—for instance, training more prop makers and elevating industrial standards for products. They have also enhanced their work in areas such as industrial design, voice acting, mold making, stage lighting and set design, and musical staging.

Tianlang asserts that what they undertake is not merely an industry, but a form of artistic creation. The purpose of these industrial breakthroughs is not to boast of technological prowess, but to utilize these means to better realize narrative arcs and convey emotion to the audience.

It is a great era, one in which we can witness the creation of many things from nothingness. Yet it is also a painful era, as we must endure the long period of pioneering development and chaos.
