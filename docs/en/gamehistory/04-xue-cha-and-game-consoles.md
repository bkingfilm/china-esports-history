# IV Xue Cha: What We’ve Missed Is More Than Just a Few Gaming Consoles

> Originally published on the Zhihu Column (2014-09-30). Original link: https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/19856097

**Behind the Scenes of Chinese Gaming History, Part IV**

### "Polygon" Xue Cha: What We’ve Missed Is More Than Just a Few Gaming Consoles

By BBKinG

On September 29, 2014, the official release of the China-region Xbox One passed with a quietude that stood in stark contrast to the mixed emotions felt by many gamers. For most gaming enthusiasts, the moment hardly stirred any excitement.

After all, what is there to celebrate when a region-locked console arrives, burdened with limited officially licensed titles and priced higher than its parallel-import counterparts?

It’s just a video game console, isn’t it? Most of us didn’t grow up playing with one, yet we’ve managed quite well without them. What exactly have we missed? It doesn’t seem to affect my life at all.

Yes, that was precisely how I felt—until I finished talking with Xue Cha.

![](../../assets/images/03df2edffb.jpg)

Xue Cha, whose real name is Yang Xuefei, was born in Wuhan in 1980. A former senior editor and reporter at *UCG*, he is widely known by his pen name, "Polygon." Having specialized in console gaming since childhood, he became the first journalist from a Chinese game media outlet dispatched to cover the E3 expo in the United States. Drawing on his firsthand experience of the domestic and international console markets’ evolution, Xue Cha was the distinguished guest at Microsoft China’s recent launch event for the Xbox One.
Xue Cha witnessed, step by step, the gradual disintegration of China’s console gaming market, while simultaneously witnessing the increasing polish of foreign titles. By contrasting these two trajectories, we gain a clearer understanding of the vast cultural chasm between Chinese and international gaming scenes: why high-quality foreign games often fail to resonate with domestic audiences, and why local developers are so relentlessly driven by quick profits.

Xue Cha’s Unusual Childhood

Xue Cha’s mother was employed at the Wuhan Friendship Store. To her generation, this name might still evoke nostalgia: these establishments were originally exclusive to foreigners residing in China before eventually opening to the general public as malls selling imported goods. Consequently, Xue Cha was exposed to a wide array of novel foreign products from an early age; he played an American Atari console as early as 1984 and experienced Nintendo’s Famicom by 1987.

Growing up in such an environment fostered in him a particular fondness for accessing the latest and finest technologies available.

Of course, video games were a universal childhood pastime. However, because personal computers were still rare in China during his teenage years, PC gaming was hardly an option. As a result, arcades and private gaming rooms became his regular haunts.

Private Gaming Rooms

This may be a term that has become somewhat hazy in many people’s memories today. Distinct from the internet cafes that emerged after 1998, these private gaming rooms specialized in console games, catering to titles ranging from the Famicom and Super Nintendo to later platforms like the PlayStation.
Inside those rooms, rows of televisions were typically lined up. Beneath each set stood a cabinet equipped with a drawer holding the game console and its controllers. Do you remember? With the drawers left slightly ajar, spilling out tangled cables and an assortment of controllers—that was our childhood.

For Xue Cha, his youth was spent in the hidden corners of Wuhan’s three towns, frequenting basement gaming arcades tucked away from public view. Coming from a modest family, he encountered consoles such as the Super Nintendo, 3DO, PlayStation, Sega Saturn, Dreamcast, and PlayStation 2 by dabbling in these small, dimly lit rooms, which were occasionally raided and shut down much like guerrilla fighters evading capture.

Compared to today, playing console games back then was quite difficult.

First, long before the state issued explicit prohibitions against game consoles in 2000, gaming machines and arcades had long been tacitly classified as "immoral venues associated with pornography, gambling, and drug use." These were unlicensed underground basements frequently raided and shut down by authorities, burdened by social stigma, which already caused a large number of console users to drop off.

Then came the high barrier to entry for console gaming. On a smaller scale, save data was always an issue. With shared consoles, saves were frequently overwritten by others. At first, I had to find ways to hide my saves; when that proved impossible, I gritted my teeth and saved up just enough to buy a memory card, which was exorbitantly expensive for a child.

On a larger scale, the Disk Station emerged during the Super Nintendo era. It is somewhat embarrassing to admit it, but its primary function was facilitating piracy: it extracted the ROMs from authentic cartridges and wrote them onto 3.5-inch floppy disks, allowing gameplay on the Disk Station without purchasing legitimate copies.
The advent of the Disk Station, coupled with the Super Nintendo’s expansive game library, accelerated console adoption—particularly for the Super Nintendo itself. Internet cafes during that era were almost exclusively outfitted with these consoles. Yet, against modern sensibilities, the cost of a Super Nintendo and Disk Station bundle in the early 1990s—roughly three to four thousand yuan—remained prohibitive for most households.

It is worth noting that the Sega Mega Drive later became more affordable, steering children from relatively well-off families toward Japanese gaming. A prime example is Gikawa Mingsheng, a Shanghai boy profiled in Chapter One of this volume’s behind-the-scenes history.

Xue Cha’s family circumstances, however, were far less fortunate.

He could only afford to play in rented computer rooms, often staying overnight to save on daily fees. At night, the proprietor would retire, locking him inside a small, dark room to play undisturbed.

In an era devoid of internet access and where game magazines were scarce, walkthroughs did not exist. Clearing levels relied entirely on exchanging tips with peers. But trapped in that solitary, darkened room, who was left to ask? Consequently, after countless failed attempts at solving puzzles, he copied down English riddles and looked them up in his home dictionary. As a result, his command of English was already exceptional by middle school, and he even picked up some Japanese during high school.

Possessing strong foreign language skills from a young age is a common trait among the many successful behind-the-scenes game developers I have interviewed.
This opportunity to step onto a larger stage came as a direct result of that initiative.

In 2002, while frequenting various gaming forums, Xue Cha was struck by a spontaneous idea: to write fan fiction for *Metal Gear Solid 2*. The piece caught the eye of "Eastern Spider," an editorial board member at *UCG*, who recommended it to *Game Person* magazine. This led to his entry into the game media industry, where he quickly garnered numerous commission requests, leveraging his deep knowledge of console games and overseas developments.

His most significant assignment came from Gouki, an editor at *UCG*, who was planning a special issue on *Metal Gear Solid* and hoped Xue Cha would author a walkthrough for the Game Boy version. Tackling the task with meticulous diligence, Xue Cha employed a laborious but effective method to create comprehensive maps for every level: he captured a screenshot after every single step taken in the emulator, then stitched the resulting images together using Photoshop.

Before he could complete this monumental project, however, fate intervened. *UCG*’s Shenzhen editorial office directly recruited him as a full-time employee. The magazine was seeking someone with robust English proficiency and a profound understanding of gaming to bolster its staff—proving the old adage that opportunity favors the prepared.

At that time, the landscape of domestic console game journalism was dominated by two major publications: *Electronic Game Software* and *UCG*. It is necessary to introduce these two titans here.
*Electronic Game Software*, launched in June 1994 under the original title *Game Concentration Camp*, holds the distinction of being China’s first official gaming magazine. However, its early years were marked by turbulence. In 1995, the publication ran a now-classic editorial titled "Crows Cawing," which lamented the plight of console gaming in China. This piece provoked the government’s stringent crackdown on consoles, leading to the magazine’s temporary suspension. It was only through a name change that *Electronic Game Software* managed to rise from the ashes. Readers are strongly encouraged to revisit this seminal article: [The Most Classic Article from Dianruan: "Crows · Cawing"](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//games.qq.com/a/20111108/000189.htm).

Two decades later, the piece’s warnings regarding the perils of game piracy and the industry’s short-sightedness remain strikingly prescient.

*UCG*, meanwhile, debuted in 1998 and evolved into a semi-monthly publication by 2001. At its zenith, its monthly circulation surpassed 300,000 copies. According to Xue Cha, joining the magazine’s editorial team in 2003 felt like entering paradise. Not only did he get to meet editors he had long admired—such as Gouki, Shaka, and Shi Shengfu—but he also gained access to a wide array of consoles that had previously been beyond his financial reach.

With free games to play and a salary to boot, what could be happier? Was there any reason not to pour himself into the work?

Soon, his dedication began to bear fruit.
By 2004, he and Gouki had been dispatched to the United States to cover the E3 gaming expo. He was only twenty-four, making it his first trip abroad. In the years that followed, even when resources were scarce, *UCG* persisted in its annual tradition of sending them to report on both the American E3 and Tokyo’s TGS. This forward-looking strategic vision delivered a wealth of firsthand intelligence to China’s burgeoning gaming industry.

Xue Cha looked back on those years with profound affection. He had never imagined that the wider world would open up to him at such a tender age. Gaining insight into the history and trajectory of home console gaming, in particular, gave him a broader perspective on the evolution of the industry as a whole.

*The Subversive History of Home Console Games*

Xue Cha posits that home console gaming originated in the United States during the 1970s. Atari’s ascent was driven not only by its visionary pivot from arcade cabinets to domestic consoles but also by its establishment of an open platform, which laid the groundwork for a rich integration of software. However, the company managed this ecosystem with surprising laxity, failing to exert effective control over game quality.

The implementation of a flawed philosophy prioritizing quantity over quality unleashed a deluge of substandard titles. This reckoning culminated in the infamous "Atari Shock" of 1983, an industry-wide collapse that left Atari abandoned by a disillusioned user base. A handful of terrible games had managed to drag down the entire North American home console market, causing Atari to plummet from its golden era.
By 1983, the industry was reeling; a handful of dreadful titles had dragged the entire North American home console market into the abyss, causing Atari’s fall from its golden age.

Nintendo recognized this lesson in hard-won hindsight. When launching the Famicom, they established a robust platform and tightened their grip on software regulation. They exerted strict control not only over game quality but also over quantity for the FC platform. This disruptive evolution significantly raised industry standards, ushering in a revival for console gaming and cementing Nintendo’s dominance in the new era.

As the old adage goes, "He who saves you is also he who destroys you."

Nintendo’s excessively harsh regulation of game developers soon pushed them to the opposite extreme.

**New Challengers: The Next-Generation Consoles**

In 1994, after Sony and Sega had launched their respective next-generation optical disc-based consoles—the PlayStation [PS] and the Sega Saturn [SS]—Nintendo stubbornly persisted with cartridges for its third-generation system, the Nintendo 64 [N64]. It clung to its archaic regime of stringent control, forcing game suppliers to utilize cartridges that were already cost-prohibitive and inefficient by comparison. This bred resistance within the industry and among players alike.

The most severe consequence was the defection of renowned developer Square, which moved *Final Fantasy VII*—originally slated for the Nintendo 64 [N64]—to Sony’s PlayStation. Given the immense cultural influence of the *Final Fantasy* series at the time and the tense standoff between Nintendo and Sony, this dealt a devastating blow to Nintendo.

Capitalizing on the momentum, Sony declared: "All games converge here."

Once again, the challenger sought to overturn Nintendo’s established platform order.
In a strategic shift, Sony maintained its oversight of game quality but loosened its grip on suppliers by permitting them to determine their own production volumes, sparing them the exorbitant costs Nintendo had previously forced upon them. Simultaneously, Sony slashed licensing fees per title, passing those savings directly to publishers and igniting a surge of enthusiasm among developers.

Meanwhile, Nintendo reeled from the catastrophic failure of the Virtual Boy, its ambitious portable 3D console that collapsed under the weight of an overly advanced concept ill-supported by contemporary technology. The debacle forced Gunpei Yokoi—architect of Nintendo’s iconic Game Boy and revered as its "father"—to resign in disgrace, dealing a crippling blow to the company’s morale and prestige.

Nintendo’s decline accelerated from that pivotal moment onward.

Yet Sony’s ascendancy was but a prelude to another seismic shift: the industry would be upended once more by the emergence of Microsoft’s Xbox in 2001—a narrative reserved for another chapter, which we shall explore in due time.
Let us turn our gaze to what was transpiring in China during this period. Throughout the 1990s, internet cafes were subject to repeated raids and closures; consumers could scarcely afford consoles, while the domestic market became awash with pirated Famicom clones and bootleg games. Sega made several attempts to penetrate the Chinese market, only to fail each time. In 1997, the joint launch of the Sega Saturn (SS) by Sega and China’s Sitong Group ultimately ended in failure. For those interested in this subject, I recommend an article titled “Because It’s Sega, Therefore It’s Gaming” [link], which offers a detailed account of Sega’s strategy in China and the historical context of that era.

Thereafter, companies attempted to skirt the edges of regulation. Take iQue, for instance, which took a convoluted path by integrating its console into a handheld device. I remain convinced that the company’s audacity had long surpassed the bounds of possibility. Not only did they secure Nintendo’s agency, but they also directly replaced the formidable Nintendo trademark with iQue’s own logo. Even in retrospect, this seems utterly unbelievable. It is akin to not only becoming the exclusive distributor of Apple iPhones in China but also replacing every Apple logo with your own profile picture—and the most astonishing part is that Apple actually agreed.

In 2003, Sony, having exhausted itself through arduous efforts, finally completed all necessary procedures and prepared to launch the domestic version of the PlayStation 2 (PS2) in China. Yet, just one day before the scheduled release, they received a mysterious notification of delay, and the console quietly hit the market six days later. This experience mirrored nearly identically what would happen with the Xbox One China launch.

However, whether these companies sought to skirt regulations or endured “the eighty-one tribulations” to obtain official authorization, they all ultimately failed.
This is because the approval process for legitimate titles has ground to a halt. Consequently, an officially licensed console in China suffers from a severe scarcity of playable games—a fatal flaw for hardware that relies on a steady stream of new releases to drive sales.

Looking back over the past thirty years, did we truly miss out only on those specific few consoles?

Xue Cha points out that due to differences in hardware support, the production standards for console games are far higher than those for PC gaming. Forty years have passed since the 1970s. The developers who grew up playing these console games abroad have now become the backbone of the modern gaming industry. How can we possibly surpass the masterpieces they created under the influence of such high-quality titles? This is, first and foremost, an issue of cultural accumulation.

Furthermore, influenced by China’s long-standing PC hardware configurations and network conditions, even if many excellent games were released here today, they would still suffer from severe "acclimatization issues." The more exquisite and complex a major title is, the faster it fails in the Chinese market. Many console games must be significantly stripped down to create online versions simply to adapt to local preferences.

So, gamers are jubilant—not because the job is done, but because this may mark the beginning of market cultivation. Although we all know in our hearts that it will take at least ten to twenty years of nurturing before we see blooming flowers and bearing fruit, at least a start has been made. This is merely the gap in cultural refinement.

The most crucial thing we might miss is likely a new wave of industrial revolution.

Today, the Xbox One—claiming to be entertainment-centric—sits in your living room. You can control it with voice commands and gesture recognition without any additional devices. It plays music and TV content, facilitates online shopping, enables video calls, and connects to a multitude of other devices.
Wait, have you seen *Iron Man*?

Do you still remember J.A.R.V.I.S., the all-knowing butler who evolved from intelligent personal assistance to military-grade applications?

Or do you believe we are merely lacking in gaming consoles?
