# 6 Huang Feng, Founder of NetFish Netcafe

**Part Six: A Hidden History of Chinese Gaming**
*From Internet Cafes to International Chains: Huang Feng, Founder of NetFish Netcafe*
By BBKinG

In October 1998, a young Huang Feng had just turned twenty. Armed with 30,000 RMB provided by his family and an additional 6,000 RMB he had borrowed himself, he purchased six computers and opened an internet cafe in the suburban district of Songjiang, Shanghai. The establishment was dedicated exclusively to gaming; it offered no general internet access.

Seventeen years later, the high-end chain brand he founded, NetFish Netcafe, has expanded to 54 cities across China. With 251 branches and a workforce of 3,000 employees, it boasts 4.8 million registered paying members. Having established outlets in Australia, Canada, and Europe, and solidifying its presence in the American market, NetFish has firmly cemented its status as China’s premier internet cafe brand.

None of this would have been possible without a casual conversation.

In 1998, Huang Feng quit his job for what felt like the umpteenth time. When his mother asked him what he had been up to lately, he replied that he had been playing games at a computer room in Songjiang Town. She then asked how much it cost per session. “Five yuan an hour,” he said. After a moment’s thought, his mother suggested, “Why don’t you open one yourself?”

Huang Feng was thrilled. He considered it a brilliant idea—not because he foresaw any grand future for the business, but simply because he realized he would no longer have to pay someone else to play games for him.

It was only later that he discovered the true brilliance of his mother’s suggestion.

He never had free time to play anymore.
Business in the computer room boomed; with six machines running from morning till night and a steady stream of patrons, Huang Feng was so consumed by managing the store that he had no time for his own gaming.

Yet, looking back, he considers this era the happiest period of his life.

Between 1998 and 2000, as China’s internet penetration accelerated rapidly, Huang Feng’s computer room transformed into an internet café. As a proprietor whose establishment never knew a dull moment, he ate and slept there daily. With few administrative burdens, his days were spare: when not tending to business, he wandered through the virtual world, playing games and browsing the web. He rode every wave of the explosive growth in esports and online gaming—*Red Alert*, *StarCraft*, *Age of Empires*, text-based MUDs, *Red Moon*, *King of Kings*, and *StoneAge*. As revenue climbed steadily, he reinvested his profits to expand his fleet—from six PCs to twelve, then twenty-four, forty-eight, and eventually ninety-six. The café’s scale doubled every six months.

He still deeply misses that feeling: doing what he loved every day while watching his enterprise grow, step by step.

Of course, such rapid expansion brought its share of troubles and trials.

For instance, lacking a proper business license, he faced regular raids. His computers were confiscated by the Administration for Industry and Commerce, and fines issued—his machines were forcibly taken away two or three times a year.

“Why didn’t you just get a license?” I asked.

Huang Feng offered a bitter smile. “I wanted to, more than anything. But at that time, the Administration for Industry and Commerce did not list ‘Internet café’ as a valid business scope. We applied, but our request was denied.”
Consequently, following each seizure of his computers, he was obliged to pay a few thousand yuan to retrieve them, allowing operations to continue. This practice only became less frequent in 2000, when licenses were finally obtainable.

From that point forward, things began to flow smoothly. Between 2000 and 2002, the shop generated daily revenues ranging from 2,000 to 8,000 yuan. With the roles of boss, network administrator, and cashier consolidated into one, his monthly expenses were confined strictly to rent and utilities. He did not even need to account for depreciation in his operational costs, as all the computers were brand new.

In 2002, Huang Feng registered his first company, Fengyu, and subsequently opened his first branch. The business entered a phase of rapid expansion, with the team size growing accordingly; it was no longer a one-person operation. To ensure healthy development, Huang Feng established several rules to guide the company’s management strategy:

1. No underage customers; strict compliance with regulations.

2. Maintain a commitment to quality.
Even from his first store, his internet cafes featured distinctive design. His pricing remained consistently higher than that of competitors, and both his amenities and service quality exceeded industry standards.

During the SARS outbreak in 2003, Fengyu’s internet cafes remained open, as many patrons continued to visit wearing masks. Although the business endured ten months of losses, the shops persisted with daily keyboard disinfection and thorough cleaning. This diligence earned special commendation from district leaders, who personally brought all the internet cafe owners from across the district for a tour.

3. Accept penalties rather than offer bribes; prevail through reason.
Huang Feng acknowledged that the internet cafe industry was rife with opportunists attempting to exploit loopholes, including minors slipping in under the radar. He did not shy away from these realities; instead, he addressed them with candor. As his business expanded, vulnerabilities were an inevitable consequence of growth. Should law enforcement catch violators in their act, he accepted the penalties with grace but drew a hard line at bribery—he refused to grease palms or smooth over difficulties through illicit means. It was a principle he held dear.

Yet, as the old adage goes, "a strong dragon cannot suppress local snakes." Every region operates by its own unwritten codes, and adhering to high principles often carries a steep price. When the company began its nationwide expansion, it encountered no shortage of local heavyweights. There were countless incidents of computers smashed by triads, visceral threats to personal safety, and veiled messages delivered through local officials urging him to leave their jurisdiction. Some competitors even stormed his office, cursing him to his face.

On several occasions, risking physical harm himself, he stood firm before these enraged rivals and declared, "Does your internet cafe truly thrive on brute force? Let us compete fairly, focusing on superior customer service and jointly elevating our brands to expand the market together."

While some continued their campaign of harassment after hearing this, others eventually became mutual friends who held him in high esteem. He always sought to persuade through reason; if that failed, he was willing to absorb the loss and withdraw. Losing millions of yuan in investment was a common occurrence during those aggressive expansion years.

In 2006, seeking to propel the company to its next stage of growth, Huang Feng registered Wangyu Company. The name emerged from an employee discussion, symbolizing their aspiration to be "a happy fish swimming freely in the vast ocean of the internet."
Huang Feng’s expansion strategy was equally unconventional: he bypassed the heart of Shanghai entirely, turning instead toward Suzhou and Nanjing. At the time, Oriental Network Point held a stranglehold on the Shanghai market. Fuelled by a surge in internet café demand and a scarcity of operating licenses, they had grown rapidly by franchising their operations. In a short span, their signage seemed to appear on every corner across the city. Yet, they committed a fatal error: by failing to implement unified management for their franchise stores, they laid the groundwork for their subsequent collapse.

The windfall of his first five million yuan prompted Huang Feng to reconsider his life’s direction.

By 2004, with seven or eight branch outlets under his belt, he suddenly realized that his earnings were not merely sufficient to purchase an apartment in downtown Shanghai and a luxury car, but ample enough to support a family. The prospect of marriage and children was within reach.

This realization struck him profoundly. It meant that for a man whose material desires were relatively modest, the struggle for survival was essentially over.

With his basic needs secured, his spiritual aspirations began to surface, casting Huang Feng into deep contemplation. What is the true value of human existence? Once you have built a home, wed, sired children, and acquired a car and an apartment, what purpose remains in continued toil?

As the company swelled in size and employed more people, he wondered: toward what destination was this entire crew sailing? What was his new life goal? These were questions he had never previously considered, yet they suddenly piled up before him with urgent clarity.

He realized that the days of being a small-time internet café owner—who needed only to sit in the shop, play games, and collect profits without thought or worry—were gone forever.

So, what should he do?

The first thing that surfaced in Huang Feng’s mind was to alter the public’s contempt for internet café operators.
Indeed, society at the time viewed internet café operators with considerable disdain. To the general public, these establishments were seedy haunts synonymous with gambling, pornography, and drug use—places where minors went to be corrupted and harmed.

Huang Feng was desperate to shift this perception. His initial approach was disarmingly simple: open a factory, engage in manufacturing, and embrace a faint echo of the old ideal of “saving the nation through industry.” But it did not work; the status quo remained unchanged.

He began reading voraciously, devouring biographies of titans like Li Ka-shing, Bill Gates, and Warren Buffett, alongside ancient historical texts, inspirational success literature, and works on religious faith. He dove into every avenue of spiritual exploration available to him. Whenever he had a free moment, he traveled the globe, visiting large corporations, scouting new technologies in Silicon Valley, and attending trade shows in New York.

It was not until one day in 2007 that someone handed him an iPhone—the first generation model—that his perspective shifted. He was stunned by its elegance; he realized that a truly great product could indeed change the world. From that moment on, he became a devotee of Steve Jobs.

Huang Feng admitted later that he is a deeply emotional man. He finished reading *Steve Jobs* in tears, absorbing each chapter with slow, deliberate reverence. He cherished the book so intensely that he was almost reluctant to let it end.

Speaking of this, he was entirely candid, his eyes holding no hint of hesitation. Such raw sensitivity seemed an unlikely trait for a boss overseeing 3,000 employees. Yet, during Jobs’s final years, Huang Feng began to forge a new worldview and value system. To change the world through product became his singular goal, a shift that allowed Wangyu to once again sync perfectly with the pulse of the times.

In 2008, China’s internet café industry was on the verge of collapse.
The reasons were myriad: aging hardware, severe market saturation, increasingly brutal price wars, and the surge in personal computer ownership, which eroded the demand for internet cafes in major metropolitan areas. The entire industry resembled a frail, shivering elder nearing the end of its days.

Countless cafés shuttered their doors, and Wangyu’s operational prospects were equally grim. Some competitors descended into ruthless, bottom-feeder price wars, slashing costs while allowing their environments and services to deteriorate into oblivion.

Yet Huang Feng believed that the public’s hunger for internet cafes remained unquenched. He foresaw the rise of esports inevitably drawing crowds seeking entertainment within such venues. The very nature of the internet cafe was shifting—transforming from a mere utility for those lacking home computers into a service-oriented hub for consumption, leisure, and social interaction. This evolution demanded higher standards for ambiance, hardware, and hospitality.

After consulting with the company, NetFish embarked on a perilous and costly experiment: opening its first flagship location on Tianyaoqiao Road in Xujiahui. They poured resources into creating an internet cafe defined by top-tier ambiance, state-of-the-art equipment, and exceptional service.

Huang Feng recognized this as truly an experiment—an audacious gamble against the tide of industry decline. Had it failed, NetFish might have vanished without a trace.

The first ten months were marked by relentless losses, a pain hard to bear. Although rent in that prime location was exorbitant, NetFish managed to cling to survival.

"Starting from the eleventh month, we saw people lining up at the entrance just to use the computers," Huang Feng said with a smile.

The experiment succeeded, and the next phase began: replication and expansion. But a new obstacle barred NetFish’s path—not enough capital.
Haunted by the memory of the Oriental Network Point’s collapse, Huang Feng was reluctant to open franchised branches; he feared it might dilute the service quality that defined their internet cafes. Consequently, by 2010, nearly all NetFish locations were wholly owned and operated by the company. Since each new establishment cost approximately three million yuan, this strategy of strict control proved prohibitively expensive.

Yet, with a clearer grasp of the market, NetFish felt compelled to accelerate its expansion to seize market share. The company set an ambitious goal: opening 300 stores within a single year. To achieve this, Huang Feng and his team toured tirelessly, seeking capital.

However, their efforts coincided with the global financial crisis of 2008–2010. Financing fell through, leaving them to open only 30 stores. Compounding these setbacks, two shareholders withdrew in succession. These trials forced Huang Feng to reconsider the viability of the franchise model.

But how could they ensure service quality among independent operators? This question initiated another arduous journey of exploration. NetFish instituted rigorous management protocols: store managers were directly appointed by headquarters, and all decoration and layout were handled centrally. These measures struck a delicate balance between rapid expansion and consistent service standards.

Huang Feng also launched new innovations. For instance, "Fish Bubble," a social service app integrated with NetFish locations, allows users to check real-time occupancy via mobile and reserve seats in advance. Additionally, on September 21, 2015, NetFish unveiled its first flagship esports hall at Beijing’s Workers' Stadium.

Though NetFish continues to face numerous challenges, Huang Feng remains relentless in his pursuit of improvement and innovation.

Huang Feng
Founder, NetFish Internet Cafes
