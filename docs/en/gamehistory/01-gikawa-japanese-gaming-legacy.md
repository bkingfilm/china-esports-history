# 1 The Japanese-Style Gaming Legacy of Zhang Mingjing ("Gikawa")

**Behind the Scenes of Chinese Gaming History: Part 1 — The Japanese-Style Gaming Legacy of Zhang Mingjing ("Gikawa")**
*By BBKinG*

Born in Shanghai in 1977 to a father who was an executive at a state-owned enterprise and a mother working as a laboratory technician, Zhang Mingjing is known to his friends simply as "Gikawa." He currently serves as Product Director at Meiyu, a subsidiary of Shengda Games. In this role, he oversees *Chain Chronicle*, a Japanese-style mobile title that has generated tens of millions in revenue and secured spots in the Apple App Store’s top ten bestseller charts on at least two occasions.

A connoisseur of both Japanese video games and history, Zhang has translated and published several renowned historical novels, including *Tokugawa Ieyasu*, *Hana no Keiji*, *RyoSEN Saku*, and *Washin Meigen Boshu*. Many readers will recognize him by his pen name: Gikawa Mingsheng.

Growing up in an affluent household, Zhang never had to worry about basic necessities. Born in a metropolis with ready access to global information, he enjoyed a childhood rich in cultural options and intellectual nourishment.

In the early 1990s, when Zhang was attending middle school, Shanghai’s Wenmiao district served as an anime and gaming sanctuary comparable to Tokyo’s Akihabara. The weekend markets there brimmed with second-hand books and manga, alongside renowned game stores stocking the latest cartridges. Although these shops still stand today, their prominence has waned in the shadow of the internet’s rise.
Yet for children growing up in Shanghai at the time, it provided a treasure trove of happy memories. New volumes of *Knights of the Zodiac* manga could be purchased as soon as they were released, and game cartridges were readily available: those for the Nintendo Family Computer—commonly known as the Famicom or “Hongbaiji” (Red and White Console)—as well as titles for the Sega Mega Drive (often called Lidaoji or MD), launched in 1988, and the Super Nintendo (Super Famicom or SFC), which followed in 1990.

Shanghai Wenmiao profoundly shaped Gikawa’s early life. His school, Shanghai Datong High School, lay close to the temple, so after classes, he and his classmates made a daily ritual of walking there to browse through manga and games. At that time, the vast majority of these works were Japanese imports, and most games lacked local translations. Consequently, deciphering Japanese became a mountain Gikawa had first to climb, serving as one of his primary motivations for studying the language.

Another significant influence on Gikawa during his youth was the practice of keeping weekly journals.

While many students are required to maintain diaries or logs throughout their school years, Zhang Mingjing’s experience differed in key respects. His classmates’ weekly journals were not graded by teachers; instead, they underwent peer review by fellow students, with the teacher offering only a final overview. This approach greatly stimulated written communication among the students and fostered an entirely new culture within their class.
Hardcover Notebook

The project began as a collaborative exercise in which the class contributed to a single hardcover volume. On the front cover, they wove an ongoing serialized narrative, with each student penning a chapter that built upon the preceding plot before passing the notebook to the next participant. The back cover served as a gallery for individual preferences, yielding a rich tapestry of genres ranging from romance and wuxia to time-travel fiction and stage plays; here, everyone shared their work and exchanged critiques.

To this day, Gikawa keeps those three high school yearbooks with meticulous care at home. A few determined writers who remained steadfast in their contributions forged lifelong bonds of friendship—ties that remain strong, sustained by frequent contact even now.

Play, Language, and Creation

These three pursuits inadvertently paved the way for his original dream: creating video games.

Though he narrowly missed admission to a Japanese literature department during university applications, Gikawa did not abandon his aspirations. He enrolled in evening classes during his spare time and, in the library of Shanghai Normal University, discovered an entire wall devoted to Japanese texts. This collection featured numerous excellent translations of Japanese novels alongside a vast array of original works in the language.

Gikawa noted that while the dialogue within games might be relatively simple, the allusions embedded in their backgrounds were profoundly complex. To truly grasp what a game was conveying required a solid command of Japanese history and culture.

Consequently, he began devouring a vast quantity of Japanese historical novels—a formidable task for an autodidact. Fortunately, while the narratives in these works were intricate, they contained far fewer loanwords than modern fiction. Much of the content relied heavily on Kanji characters, allowing him to infer meanings as he read.
In this way, Gikawa managed to read nearly three hundred Japanese books. The most impressive among them were two novels by the author Ryohta Sima—*Sekigahara* and *Josai*—which vividly depicted the Battle of Sekigahara and the Siege of Osaka Castle during Japan’s Sengoku period.

*Sega’s Sakura Wars*

This was the game Gikawa played during his university years, one that would repeatedly alter the course of his subsequent fate. Even years later, whenever *Sakura Wars* was mentioned, Gikawa’s eyes would light up. He spoke of its exceptionally rich character designs, noting that each figure was distinct enough to win a player’s affection for at least one. Moreover, it revolutionaryily and seamlessly blended strategy simulation (SLG) mechanics with role-playing game (RPG) narratives, creating an incredibly immersive experience. He spent hundreds of hours playing through every character arc.

In 1998, after graduating, he began combining his hobbies and strengths by writing anime fanfiction as well as articles on gaming and history. He is said to have been the first person to publish such fanfiction in Chinese periodicals. For a long time, he contributed to print media outlets such as *Computer & Consumer Electronics Information*, to the point where he briefly considered living off his writing royalties—only for reality’s instability to quickly shatter that dream.

By 2000, after moving around a bit, he received an opportunity to work as an IT magazine editor in Beijing. Although he still hoped to pursue a career in the gaming industry, he knew that Beijing was then the best place for such an atmosphere, so he went without hesitation, moved into a basement, and became one of the *Beipiao*—the floating population in Beijing.

Fate can be so dramatic at times.
Three months later, the IT magazine folded. Zhang was forced to move on, joining another firm as an editor for its gaming website. That company soon announced it would serve as the agent for titles from Sega of Japan, including *Sakura Wars*, which happened to be Gikawa’s favorite and most familiar game. From that point forward, the company transformed itself entirely into a game publisher and operator, growing ever larger; after Zhang had left, it even secured agency rights for the famous online title *Mortal Online*.

The company was known as Tianren Hudong.

Tianren Hudong exerted a profound influence on China’s gaming community, giving rise to many legendary figures in the modern industry. Among them were Wang Tao, CEO of Sohu Changyou, and Li Haiyi, Senior Vice President of Perfect World.

Although Gikawa would later achieve success of his own, those days at Tianren Hudong found him merely an ordinary member of the marketing department.

We often say that opportunity favors the prepared mind, but this is only half true. It favors those who are not only prepared but proactive.

Zhang Mingjing was precisely such a person. Upon learning that his company had secured the agency rights for the PC version of Sega’s *Sakura Wars*, he stepped forward to offer his help. By then, the Taiwanese branch had already completed the localization work. However, after a careful review, Zhang realized that the translation differed significantly from the linguistic habits of mainland China. He approached the project manager and proposed a complete re-localization. Touched by his earnestness, the company agreed to let him take a crack at it.

For the next two-plus months, Zhang not only fulfilled his regular duties but also seized every spare moment to translate the documentation, which totaled 500,000 to 600,000 words. He worked day and night, translating and researching references in equal measure. Though exhausted, he was incredibly happy. What joy could compare to participating in one’s own dream?

In August 2001, Tianren Hudong released the official Chinese version of *Sakura Wars*, shifting a hundred thousand copies in sales. They followed this with both a standard edition and a TV-compatible version. The impact on players was immense; in an era rife with piracy, such a feat was nothing short of astonishing.

During his stint as a "Beipiao" in Beijing, Gikawa had cultivated several close friendships through the craft of game writing: Murasame, Salala, cOMMANDO, and God Favors Infantry—who was also a co-founder of CSCHINA—all of whom were prominent figures in the industry at the time. The group worked together at Yalian Games and shared a rental apartment in Beijing’s Ganyuan Residential Area. Almost every weekend, Zhang would join them for gaming sessions and archery practice, spending the night on their living room floor, lending their quarters a distinct dormitory atmosphere.

Gikawa later crossed paths with another colleague from Yalian Games. This individual became a pivotal force in his life, serving as both mentor and friend: none other than Chen Guang, who would later rise to the position of Vice President at Tencent Shanghai.

By 2003, Zhang Mingjing returned to Shanghai to join Tencent Shanghai’s overseas business development team, where she took charge of importing Japanese titles.

After years of wandering and striving, she finally took a step closer to her dream of creating games herself. In 2004, Sega of Japan approached Tencent again to negotiate game distribution rights. Naturally, Gikawa served as the official point of contact. Through his interactions representing Tencent with Sega’s representatives, his profound knowledge of Japanese culture and encyclopedic familiarity with their back catalog left an indelible impression.
Consequently, when Sega of Japan established its China branch to implement its gaming platform strategy later that same year, Gikawa received a formal invitation. For someone who had grown up playing Sega games, this was a long-awaited and supremely glorious opportunity. Without much hesitation, he accepted the offer. Upon joining Sega Network China, he served as a game producer for *Sakura Wars Online* and *Saint Seiya Online*, titles that had only recently been proposed.

Looking back, Gikawa felt that this career move was somewhat unfair to his old friend, Chen Guang. Fortunately, Chen harbored no ill will; indeed, he would go on to be a benefactor once again many years later.

Gikawa had originally believed that his tenure at Sega China would become the most cherished period of his life. Instead, to his surprise, it evolved into a chapter he was reluctant to discuss in detail. He summarized those two years with a phrase born of disappointment and frustration: a scenario where Japanese managers, lacking understanding of China, were led by Koreans who shared that same ignorance, directing Chinese employees in developing marketing strategies and game content—a setup destined for failure.
By mid-2006, Gikawa had resigned, driven by a mixture of disillusionment and anger. Shortly thereafter, Sega Networks China shuttered its operations. Subsequent media reports shed light on the internal dynamics, clearly identifying an "inability to adapt" as the primary cause of the collapse.

His childhood dream seemed tantalizingly within reach, only to slip through his fingers once more. The relentless blows of such setbacks had left Gikawa deeply despondent. Undeterred by the failure, he founded a small company upon resigning. Leveraging the professional networks he had cultivated over years in both the Chinese and Japanese gaming industries, he worked as an intermediary—facilitating introductions, managing game publishing agencies, and recruiting talent for headhunting firms. Though profits were meager, Gikawa quietly accumulated resources while biding his time for another opportunity to return to game development.

In 2010, Apple released the iPad.

Gikawa believed that mobile games would soon become the mainstream. He devised a strategy to enter the market using dynamic comics as a wedge; once he had established intellectual property rights and anime-style characters, he could pivot toward game development.

This was an indirect path to self-developed games—a strategy Gikawa felt compelled to adopt. At the time, the barrier to entry for game development remained prohibitively high, making external investment the only viable route for independent success. Consequently, he purchased the manga license for *Ghost Blows Out the Light*, developed a Motion Comic app, and even commissioned an original score. Just as his initial investments began to show promise, another fully animated comic project was rejected during Apple’s review process. This setback ultimately derailed his subsequent funding efforts, leading to failure.
Years of accumulated expertise were once again squandered, erasing his ability to return to the comfortable life he had known before starting a family. With a wife and daughter to support, he could not simply yield to fate. Driven by the welfare of his household and the persistence of his own ambitions, thirty-five-year-old Gikawa resolved to try once more.

In 2012, Chen Guang, then Vice President of Shengda Games, recruited Gikawa. Soon after, Gikawa established contact with Sega in Japan. Fortune favored him; Sega was preparing to debut several new titles in China for ChinaJoy, including *Chain Chronicle*, which had not yet seen release in Japan at that time.

Today, Gikawa has realized his dream of game development and gained profound industry insight. As anticipated, the mobile gaming landscape has grown fiercely competitive, particularly with the recent surge in card-based titles. Leveraging his extensive experience with mature Japanese-style card games, Gikawa penned three articles detailing his findings: *General Theory of Card Games*, *How to Let More People Draw Cards*, and *Trading System of Cards*. These works earned him the recognition of numerous industry professionals. He also shared operational insights, such as his observation that in-game promotional events are a double-edged sword requiring delicate handling.

Regarding his future plans, Gikawa expressed a desire to continue developing domestically produced games. While foreign titles possess many merits, they do not fully align with the habits and needs of the domestic market. He believes we must absorb their strengths—not only the high production standards of Japanese games but also the grand perspective of Western ones—and integrate Chinese culture with player demands. This approach enables faster operational responses and adjustments, ensuring the long-term development of our products.
