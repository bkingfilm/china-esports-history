**Youku Is Not a Media Outlet: Game Editor-in-Chief Xiucai**
*By BBKinG*

Xiucai (Zhou Ye), currently the Game Editor-in-Chief at Youku, was born on July 14, 1981, in Wuxi, Jiangsu. From a young age, he harbored a deep love for literature and history. His family’s extensive book collection spanned a wide array of subjects; he excelled particularly in classical Chinese texts and held *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* in especially high regard. It was for this reason that he earned the nickname "Xiucai."

A sudden whim led him to enroll in the Directing Department at the Shanghai Theatre Academy.

In 2000, on the eve of the college entrance examination, Xiucai watched a TV drama set against the backdrop of the Shanghai Theatre Academy. Completely captivated by the story, he suddenly felt that the institution was quite intriguing, which sparked his desire to apply. However, art academies accepted only arts majors, while he had been a science student at the time. The question of whether a science student could apply became the first hurdle he faced after persuading his parents. Upon inquiry, he discovered an option on the college entrance application form labeled "Arts and Science." As long as he selected that box, he would be eligible to apply. Thus, after much hassle, Xiucai became the first science student in a century at his Shanghai high school to switch to the arts.

The arts entrance examination was arduous; merely being permitted to sit for it was but the opening of the door.
Which major should he pursue? Xiucai pored over the Shanghai Theatre Academy’s recruitment brochure, studying it until the pages nearly blurred.

Most arts disciplines demanded years of specialized training. After weighing his options, he came to realize that only one path seemed remotely viable for him: the Department of Directing. Its prerequisites appeared broad rather than deep; it required a smattering of knowledge across the board, but mastery in none.

With self-deprecating humor, Xiucai mused that perhaps those who knew nothing at all should simply apply for directing. Yet to assume the directing examination was a mere formality would be a grave error. Vague criteria breed fierce competition. That year, nearly two thousand candidates vied for admission to the college’s directing department, yet only sixteen were accepted. Moreover, his competitors were not novices like himself; they were seasoned professionals with rich life experience and backgrounds in film or television, often older than he was. As a recent high school graduate facing off against middle-aged practitioners, Xiucai’s chances of success were slim indeed.

Furthermore, admission to the directing department required surviving three rigorous stages.

The first stage tested improvisation on concrete topics. Candidates were presented with ten specific prompts, chosen one, given fifteen minutes to prepare, and then tasked with performing a five-minute sketch. While this component might have been manageable for students with formal art training, it was an insurmountable hurdle for Xiucai, who possessed no such background. Fortunately, he scraped through by the skin of his teeth.
The second stage tested abstract topic improvisation. Xiucai was assigned the theme of “Black.” Here, you might pause to wonder: if it were you, how would you embody such an intangible concept?

The next round featured ten highly imaginative questions designed to probe the breadth of one’s knowledge and social experience. For Xiucai, who was still quite young, this initially seemed a formidable challenge, but his habit of reading widely proved to be of great assistance.

Let us now consider how Xiucai handled his chosen topic, “Black.”

He quickly composed and performed a skit titled *“Sudden Power Outage During Evening Self-Study in Senior Year.”* In under five minutes, he captured a subtle yet tense slice of life: the class immersed in their work, busy and oppressive; the confusion, darkness, screams, and sudden release during the blackout; the awkwardness that followed upon restoration; and the eventual return to the grind.

The third round tested film criticism. A movie was screened on-site—*Ambush*, starring Feng Gong—and participants were required to write their reviews immediately afterward. This task proved particularly manageable for him.

Ultimately, Xiucai secured admission to the Directing Department at Shanghai Theatre Academy, achieving a national ranking of fifth place in this artistic institution founded in the 1940s.

At this point, many readers might ask: What does writing about this have to do with the behind-the-scenes history of games?

Whether there is a connection will be discussed later; for now, simply keep reading.

When the academic term began, Xiucai embarked on his freshman year at the Shanghai Theatre Academy. His cohort of sixteen students was split into two classes, with each instructor mentoring eight pupils. While they studied a broad range of film and television disciplines, the curriculum did not delve as deeply as specialized tracks might have. The first two years were dedicated entirely to acting; it was only in their third and fourth years that they attended classes alongside students from other departments. The pedagogical philosophy was clear: cultivate generalists rather than specialists. Just as one trains a general manager or a military officer to see the bigger picture, so too must directors master the overview without becoming bogged down in narrow technicalities.

Consequently, graduates of the directing department are equipped to navigate various roles across the film and television industry. Xiucai observed that many actors now held in high esteem graduated from this very department, yet the reverse is far less common; individuals are often constrained by the specific expertise they have cultivated. Each year, the Shanghai Theatre Academy hosts a collaborative graduation showcase featuring productions by both the directing and acting departments. Both groups invite students from other majors within their graduating class to co-create these shows, making the event an annual highlight.

By the time the senior year’s graduation productions concluded, only twelve of Xiucai’s original sixteen classmates remained; the attrition rate was, as they say, ruthless.

The Gaming Connection

During his freshman year, drawn by his passion for video games, Xiucai met Zhang Rui—who would later become a producer at Game TV and was a year ahead of him in school. This chance encounter laid the groundwork for his eventual entry into the gaming industry.
Xiucai was persuaded by a lighting-design major in his senior year to play *RO*, which led him on to *Mortal Online* and *Ultima Online*. Through these titles, he cultivated a wide network of friends within the gaming community. Regarding that community’s evolution, Xiucai observes that the first generation of Chinese guild players emerged from *Ultima Online* in 2003, followed by a second cohort in *Mortal Online*. The third wave coalesced during the eras of *Lineage II* and *WoW*. Many of today’s industry leaders rose to prominence during those formative years; indeed, history itself forged those heroes.

**Channel[G] Game Channel**

After graduating in 2004, Xiucai joined Mingri Technology’s Channel[G], where he managed online game news and esports programming. At the time, this channel produced content for the “Game Wind” channel on Shanghai Media Group’s Internet Interactive TV platform.

It was during this period that Xiucai met Yao Mo (former Deputy General Manager of Game Wind), Sun Jun (Head of the Website Department at Game Wind), Wu Qiang, Wen Shengke, and others who would later become founders of GamesTV.

**Joining GamesTV**

Following ChinaJoy in 2005, Zhang Rui recommended Xiucai for a position at GamesTV—which was later merged with Game Wind—to oversee online game programming. While Zhang Rui handled single-player game content and Yao Mo directed esports programming, Xiucai’s focus lay elsewhere. Prior to 2007, he had worked exclusively as a director and producer, creating guides, feature specials, and programs exploring the cultural origins of *World of Warcraft*. After 2007, he transitioned into sales. Thanks to his background in direction, he possessed broad industry knowledge and a deep familiarity with games, allowing him to excel in this new role.
During this era, live-streaming technology advanced rapidly. Platforms such as PPS and PPLive emerged, while MediaCode streaming technology began to mature. Content creators could simply broadcast their feeds to professional platforms like PPS, which would then manage the infrastructure required to support massive simultaneous audiences.

In early 2006, *StarsWar 2* achieved monumental success by broadcasting live across China, drawing 200,000 unique IP addresses and over two million viewers—a triumph fueled directly by these technological breakthroughs.

Xiucai observed that such technological revolutions had driven several periods of explosive growth in esports. From 1998 to 2005, Chinese audiences were largely unable to watch major esports events live; they relied entirely on textual descriptions and still images. Video streaming remained a global technical bottleneck during this period, so much so that it even necessitated the rise of audio-only streaming sites.

In 2004, users attempted live broadcasting via Microsoft Media Player—a cumbersome workaround for early video transmission. The system was inefficient: a 1 Mbps bitstream required 1 Mbps of bandwidth per viewer. Two viewers demanded 2 Mbps; one hundred required 100 Mbps. Given the low broadband penetration and exorbitant costs of the time, no operator could sustain such scaling. More critically, the underlying technology simply could not handle multiple concurrent users.

Public demand for content ultimately drove technological innovation, which in turn expanded the reach of that content. The entirety of gaming history has been propelled by this interplay between user appetite and technical capability.
The Rivalry of Content and Technology

Technological evolution has further amplified the reach of content. Indeed, the entire arc of gaming history has been driven by the dynamic tension between these two forces.

**Youku Gaming’s Technological Breakthrough**

Founded in 2006, Youku steadily refined its streaming-on-demand service through continuous improvements in CDN network acceleration. Against this backdrop, China’s gaming landscape underwent profound transformation: broadband penetration rates surged, and competitive gaming gained momentum.

In 2008, as Youku relocated its headquarters from Beijing to establish a presence in Shanghai, Xiucai entered the picture. Around this time, Xiucai’s perception of the industry shifted dramatically. Prior to 2008, he had believed that content was king—that producers held the reins. Afterward, however, he realized that platform was king, and that distribution channels wielded true power. Consequently, he began seeking suitable platforms for collaboration.

He noted that Tudou’s structure remained unspecialized; its gaming channel was merely an auto-generated archive of user uploads. In contrast, Youku had already begun curating content. Yet, because Tudou had entered the market earlier and operated on a larger scale, it enjoyed entrenched user loyalty. As the saying goes, consumer habits form a mountain difficult to move.

**Joining Youku**

In 2009, Xiucai joined Youku to lead marketing and planning for its gaming sector. That June, after Tudou poached the original chief editor of Youku’s gaming division, Xiucai assumed the role of chief editor for the channel himself.
Xiucai described his initial state as one of standing in a wasteland. Compared to Tudou, Youku held no advantage; its rival possessed richer content and had drawn away the top experts and influencers. Meanwhile, Youku’s gaming division was still in its infancy, with former staff having taken valuable resources over to their new employer. Faced with this deficit, Xiucai knew he had to carve out a new path.

**Rebuilding Youku Gaming Through Esports Content**

Drawing on his extensive experience in the esports sphere from his time at *Gaming Fengyun* (Game Star), Xiucai conducted a thorough analysis and decided to pivot away from traditional online games, focusing instead on esports programming. He recognized that in 2009, DOTA was on an upward trajectory, and user demand for tutorials and related videos was rising steadily.

To capitalize on this trend, Xiucai rallied existing DOTA creators, identifying Shuizi and Jiayan as two figures with strong user bases, and subsequently recruited new talent such as DZ and Haitao. He directed all of the channel’s promotional resources toward them and leveraged his established connections within the esports community to coordinate joint promotions on platforms like [Replays.net](http://link.zhihu.com/?target=http%3A//Replays.net). This strategy created a momentum that began to snowball rapidly.
Six months later, Xiucai’s Youku Gold Commentary team had doubled its ranks from five to ten members. By then, DOTA was surging in popularity across China. Whether the game was driving video consumption or the videos were fueling the game’s fame remained unclear, but on-demand viewership of commentary content exploded in 2010, climbing from tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand views. Gradually, an audience had formed, cultivating a habit of watching match replays.

Consequently, when Haitao began uploading videos to Youku at the end of 2010, he reached 300,000 views within just three or four months. By mid-2009—half a year later—he had already surpassed the initial milestone of 500,000 clicks. This rapid expansion was deeply rooted in the groundwork laid in the preceding years.

The stars aligned; favorable timing, location, and circumstance became the forces that propelled him forward.

On December 8, 2010, Youku went public on the New York Stock Exchange. An influx of capital accelerated the platform’s development, continuously refining video streaming quality and user experience. While DOTA remained fiercely popular, Xiucai had already begun quietly preparing content for League of Legends.

Youku attracted a massive wave of new commentators, who gained significant visibility through the platform. As commercialization efforts—such as those on Taobao—improved, commentator incomes stabilized. With production barriers lowered, more people entered the field, driving esports into a period of rapid growth from 2010 to the present day.

Against the backdrop of this grand era, many rose to prominence by being in the right place at the right time and doing the right thing—a destiny that included Xiucai himself.
In the five years since, a host of prominent commentators have risen through the ranks at Youku. Among its most celebrated talents—Haitao, 2009, Xiao Zhi, Xiao Cang, and Xiao Mo—each received crucial support from Xiucai during their formative years.

**Youku Is a Platform, Not a Media Outlet**

One might argue that the surging demand for esports video content, coupled with Youku’s vast library of resources, played a pivotal role in advancing the industry. Commentary, as a nascent profession, found its footing on this platform, acting as a vital conduit between audiences and players alike. With this channel established, the long-standing challenge of monetizing esports viewership began to yield to solution, rendering commentary one of the most viable post-retirement careers for professional players.

Yet Xiucai maintains that Youku is not a media outlet in the traditional sense, but rather a stage designed for every commentator to shine. Nevertheless, the emergence of commentators as independent creators has significantly eroded the discourse power of traditional media; in some instances, it has even compelled legacy outlets to rely on the content generated by top-tier commentators. It is an undeniable reality that the influence of leading commentators often eclipses that of certain esports websites—a dynamic keenly felt by numerous niche platforms.

How, then, should one balance the relationship between media, commentators, and platforms? This question has emerged as a fresh challenge in the wake of this business model’s growth.

**New Technological Bottlenecks**
Xiucailso noted that, amidst the prevailing surge of independent media, Youku Gaming is actively recalibrating its strategy while remaining optimistic about the proliferation of mobile devices. Yet numerous hurdles remain on the horizon: How can mobile network speeds be enhanced? How might exorbitant data costs be mitigated? How can services cater to audiences seeking more leisurely content consumption? And how can the demand for rapid news reporting be met? At present, these challenges are constrained by new technological bottlenecks.

In other words, a fresh wave of technological transformation may be on the verge of breaking. Who will be the first to crack this technical impasse and seize the opportunity? Who shall it be?
