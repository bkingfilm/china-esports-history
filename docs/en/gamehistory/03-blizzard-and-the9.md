# Part III: The Truth Is, Blizzard Never Wanted to Divorce Jiucheng

> Originally published on the Zhihu Column (2014-09-19). Original link: https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/19849790

**Behind the Scenes of Chinese Gaming History: Part III**

**Blizzard Never Wanted to End Its Marriage with Jiucheng: The Untold Story Behind *World of Warcraft***

By BBKinG

When one traces the annals of China’s gaming industry, few case studies are as foundational as the operational history of *World of Warcraft* in China. It stands as the quintessential example among them.

In 2004, American Blizzard Entertainment approached *World of Warcraft*, then still in closed beta testing, with considerable hesitation. This caution stemmed from a series of high-profile failures of large-scale online games in China, such as *Mortal Online* and *EverQuest*. The market had proven unforgiving, and Blizzard was skeptical about whether a similar title could succeed there. Yet, against these odds, the game would go on to build a digital super-empire across the globe. It did not merely become an industry benchmark; it ignited an unprecedented ecosystem of ancillary gaming cultures and industries. Furthermore, it bestowed both fame and immense fortune upon The Ninth City Group (Jiucheng), its domestic publisher, catapulting them into the ranks of China’s elite game companies.

By 2009, the "divorce" between Blizzard and Jiucheng erupted into chaos. The conflict was so tumultuous that it escalated to direct confrontations between high-level regulatory bodies, specifically the Ministry of Culture and the Press and Publication Administration. During this period, the open and covert struggle between the two parties was fierce—a battle for survival as intense as any spy thriller drenched in blood and fire. It even gave rise to an underground proxy system, epitomized by figures such as "Uncle Party."
Times have changed, however. By 2014, Blizzard and NetEase had settled into a stable phase of their relationship. The merger of the Press and Publication Administration with the State Administration of Radio, Film and Television in 2013 to form the National Press and Publication Administration had brought regulatory calm, and *World of Warcraft* was poised to release version 6.0. Everything seemed to have stilled into a deceptive tranquility.

Yet history cannot be evaded or ignored.

Did you know that Blizzard initially had no desire to sever ties with Jiucheng?

Indeed, at Blizzard’s final special meeting held at its U.S. headquarters in 2008, the prevailing sentiment was still one of continued partnership. So why did they ultimately part ways? Who placed the final straw on the camel’s back? Who pushed the other into a corner? Was it Jiucheng’s strategic missteps, or Blizzard’s fit of rage?

Let us begin at the start.

In April 2004, The Ninth City Group signed a strategic cooperation agreement with Blizzard Entertainment (under Vivendi Universal Game), securing exclusive operational rights for *WOW* in mainland China.

At that time, Sun Tao led this project as Jiucheng’s CTO. He is credited with the feat of bringing *WOW* to Jiucheng and served as the first project leader. Known for his striking good looks, he was jokingly dubbed “the handsomest man in the industry” by the gaming community. (He later left Jiucheng to found IceMotion Games, sold the company a few years later, and now works at Ping An China.)
The other two pivotal figures were Product Director Huang Lingdong and Technical Director Jiang Huanxin.

Huang, the inaugural member of Jiucheng’s virtual community and a founding veteran of the company (later promoted to Vice President of Products at Jiucheng, and currently Deputy General Manager of the Product Department for Tencent Shanghai Interactive Entertainment), personally spearheaded the *WoW* project team. He conducted the direct interviews that brought on many of its early recruits. These included Ma Gang, the project manager responsible for external communications (now Senior Marketing Director at Ubisoft China); Liu Feng, known by his handle "Aya," who managed media relations (now Vice President at Hangzhou Legong); and Ediart Tian Jian, creator of the *Azeroth National Geography* website and its localization lead (who founded Shanghai Youdian Information Technology in 2011 to develop mobile games). Additionally, Wu Jian, a veteran of the *WoW* project whose tenure spanned the histories of both Jiucheng and Blizzard China, initially oversaw planning and operations. He later joined Blizzard China to manage the operations of *WoW* and other titles before being invited to serve as COO of KongNet.

Jiang Huanxin, the Technical Director, came from HP and joined Jiucheng at the inception of the *WoW* project. He subsequently served as Vice President of Jiucheng, left the company for a period, and has recently resurfaced to assist in its expansion into the North American market.
At that time, Jiucheng lacked a dedicated marketing department, relying solely on one large product division. The company initially outsourced its website development to 01media, a team that has since grown into a renowned design firm. Later, they hired Zhou Hao ZAX to manage the site; he now serves as CEO of Xima Company, which operates in the esports sector and owns brands such as WE Club and StarsWar. Another staff member was web designer Hu Yilin, who later established himself as a prominent independent designer.

The original WoW team comprised roughly ten individuals (please forgive the gaps in my research; the record regarding certain personnel may contain inaccuracies or omissions that I intend to correct over time). By the end of 2004, however, the team structure remained unchanged.

Once the World of Warcraft development unit was established, translation and localization emerged as the game's top priority. The initial release required over one million words to be translated, prompting the recruitment of six or seven part-time translators to get the project off the ground. To facilitate their efforts, Jiucheng relocated the localization team from Sino Land to the quieter Calder Building next door, even reserving a conference room for them to work late into the night.

Liu Jilei (who later joined Tencent to handle mobile integration) conducted the first round of proofreading, followed by Wu Jian in the second. Tian Jian handled the third round and final polishing. His contributions were particularly vital; drawing on his profound understanding of Western gaming culture, he transformed stiff, literal translations into elegant, cohesive prose—work that earned widespread acclaim from players down the line.
For the Chinese voice dubbing, Ni Kang—a renowned director at the Shanghai Film Dubbing Studio with a distinguished background in voice acting—was brought on as the voice direction lead. He would later go on to serve as the Chinese voice director for *League of Legends*.

Ni Kang was nothing if not meticulous. He recruited top-tier talent from across the country, summoning them to Shanghai for the recording sessions. Among them, seeking a truly domineering presence for the game’s great bosses, Nefarian and Ragnaros, he specifically sought out Mongolian voice actor Gong Ge'er, arranging for him to travel overnight by train to Shanghai.

The following morning, Gong Ge'er stepped directly into the booth to begin recording. Yet, after hours of attempts, the team realized the young man simply wasn’t nailing it. By noon, the project team deliberated and politely offered to buy him a meal—a tacit signal that it might be time to part ways.

But who could have guessed that after the meal, Gong Ge'er’s energy and spirit surged back, along with his commanding presence? In just two continuous hours, he completed all the voiceover work for the great bosses. Consequently, many of those insufferably taunting lines players heard during repeated raid wipes were likely delivered by Gong Ge'er, fueled by a post-meal adrenaline rush.

As closed beta preparations, launch logistics, government approvals, and even Coca-Cola collaborations mounted, these became the key priorities leading up to the open beta in 2005. While the outside world remained pessimistic about *World of Warcraft*, Jiucheng’s internal confidence continued to grow.

When the initial closed beta keys were first distributed, more than 300,000 Blizzard fans applied within mere days. The criteria were stringent, requiring extensive user information and documentation, yet the WoW project team noted with admiration how meticulously players filled out their forms.
World of Warcraft’s burgeoning online community soon began producing content of remarkable quality. Veteran players may still recall the video titled *Kangxi Wants to Play WoW*, a production of exceptional polish that, shortly after its debut in 2005, amassed hundreds of thousands of views. Impressed by the fan-made masterpiece, staff from Jiucheng tracked down the creator and presented them with several CD keys as a token of appreciation.

On April 9, 2005, marking the inaugural “World of Warcraft Day,” Jiucheng hosted a player meet-and-greet at Shanghai’s Luwan Gymnasium to announce the open beta launch date. Heavy rain fell that day, yet thousands turned out anyway. The venue was packed to capacity, while countless others waited outside in the downpour. This display of devotion gave the WoW development team immense confidence and encouragement. Rumor has it that Jiucheng’s CEO, Zhu Jun, also attended; he stepped into the arena for a brief glance, offered a knowing smile, and returned backstage, already certain of the game’s success.

Although the public beta launch date was announced as April 29, it actually began on April 26.

On the night the closed beta concluded, Jiucheng staff organized an event dubbed “Invasion of the Demon World's End.” The operations team took control of major monsters to interact with players in the main cities, dancing and celebrating alongside them. While Blizzard typically restricts Game Masters from controlling NPCs, this rule was suspended for that evening, making the event a rare occurrence. For those fortunate enough to participate, it remains a wonderful, memorable chapter in gaming history.

A brief aside is warranted here: prior to the public beta launch, operators prepared installation discs for *World of Warcraft*. The master disc used to create these copies was a closely guarded secret of Blizzard’s, containing the most complete version of the game files—including the latest translations and all in-game content.
Unsatisfied with trusting courier services, Blizzard dispatched an employee, Stan Wang, to personally transport the discs from the United States. The Jiucheng development team was so thrilled that they pulled an all-nighter, waiting in anticipation for their arrival. Yet, when the package finally arrived and they eagerly inserted the disc into their machines, they discovered that the master copy had been damaged—a deeply ironic turn of events.

The public beta had arrived; at long last, it was here.

Though the level cap was set at 45, player enthusiasm far outstripped expectations. Every server faced massive queues. At that time, Blizzard’s European and American branches had promoted the slogan "A world awaits," which Chinese players humorously dubbed "A world of waiting." The public beta testing queues for World of Warcraft exerted a profound influence on subsequent gaming titles, evolving into a cultural phenomenon. Some industry observers noted that later games even simulated the experience of queuing even when no actual wait was necessary.

**Blizzard's Dominance**

Blizzard imposed stringent requirements on Jiucheng regarding the operation of World of Warcraft, some of which were quite rigorous. For instance, under Blizzard’s directives, Jiucheng purchased state-of-the-art servers from HP—the most advanced available at the time. Reports suggested that the computational power of this entire server cluster far surpassed that of the Tianhe supercomputer.

Blizzard’s motivation was straightforward: to provide players with the absolute best gaming experience possible. However, such assertive practices often placed operators in difficult positions, laying the groundwork for future rifts between the two companies.

Soon, the number of participants in the public beta exceeded 600,000.
A palpable sense of elation swept through the project team; beyond the sheer satisfaction of their achievement, everyone was eager to collect Boss Zhu Jun’s promised bonus. Earlier that year, in early 2005, Zhu had declared that if player numbers surpassed 500,000, a $500,000 reward would be issued.

Yet many departed before they ever saw that money, sowing the seeds for a subsequent exodus of key team members.

By 2005, Jiucheng was ambitiously charting a new strategic course. It began aggressively acquiring a portfolio of overseas titles—*Kunai*, *Miracle World*, *Hellgate: London*, *Guild Wars*—among others. This dual-purpose strategy aimed both to stifle domestic competition by securing the best games first and to free the company from being forever mere puppets of Blizzard.

From a strategic standpoint, this pivot was sound, particularly as Shanda’s experience clearly indicated that friction between agency firms and development houses would only intensify; preparation was paramount. However, Jiucheng faced a different adversary than Shanda had: not a Korean rival, but the veteran gaming behemoth, Blizzard Entertainment.

Tensions flared almost immediately at the close of 2005. The foreign side was preparing to launch *The Burning Crusade*, an expansion packed with new technologies. The U.S. team argued that superior server support was essential to maintain an optimal gaming experience and insisted Jiucheng upgrade its infrastructure.

This was a bitter pill to swallow: Jiucheng had just recently spent a fortune on servers that far outstripped those at Yinhe, and they had scarcely been put into service! Hesitant to invest again so soon, Jiucheng stalled. The rift widened further.
Consequently, Blizzard accelerated its strategy to penetrate the Chinese market directly, laying the groundwork in 2005 to establish a local subsidiary, Blizzard China.

It was evident that both parties were locked in a game of chess. Truthfully, however, there was no clear moral high ground; each side was merely fighting for survival, playing by the ruthless laws of the jungle.

By 2006, Blizzard had intensified its expansion efforts within China. At this juncture, Jiucheng executed a rather peculiar maneuver: it began siphoning core members of the *World of Warcraft* project team away from their primary game to oversee various newly acquired titles. Simultaneously, it redeployed its internal R&D personnel to handle the day-to-day operations of *WoW*.

I suspect this move served multiple purposes. First, it allowed experienced veterans to guide these new games; second, it offered Jiucheng’s own R&D staff a chance to mature through practical experience. There was likely a third, more political motive: to prevent the pro-Blizzard faction within the *WoW* team from accumulating too much power.

Whose idea this strange maneuver was remains unknown, as does its accuracy. But I wager that whoever devised it never imagined it would backfire with the force of the Seven Wounds Fist—injuring both the target and the thrower.

Sun Tao, the project team head and a key figure in the pro-Blizzard faction, was the first to leave, resigning to found his own company while taking a contingent of employees with him. Jiucheng then appointed Nancy Zhou (Zhou Ning) as its new COO. Compared to Sun Tao, she adopted a far tougher stance toward Blizzard. (Nancy currently serves as General Manager of Ubisoft China.)

Meanwhile, Wu Jian, Jiucheng’s head of operations, was recruited by Blizzard to become its fourth employee at Blizzard China, having refused to work on anything other than *WoW*.
Zhu Jun was incensed, preparing to dispatch legal notices to both Wu Jian and Blizzard. During an internal company meeting, he issued a stern decree: no employee was permitted any form of contact with Wu Jian, nor could Blizzard allow him to interfere in any work related to *World of Warcraft* (WoW).

The conflict escalated significantly, eventually forcing Jiucheng and Blizzard to sign a non-solicitation agreement, which gradually helped to defuse the tension. What appeared on the surface to be merely an employee changing jobs was, in reality, a clash of strategic interests between two corporations.

At that time, Michael Fong served as Blizzard’s General Manager—a Hong Kong native with Canadian citizenship. One of his primary responsibilities was establishing Blizzard’s subsidiary in China, a task Wu Jian had begun researching upon Fong’s arrival.

In the years that followed, just as the bond between *World of Warcraft* and its Chinese player base grew stronger, so too did the rift between Jiucheng and Blizzard. On one hand, Blizzard sought greater involvement in operations and deeper insight into operational details; on the other, Jiucheng refused to update its servers and kept critical data concealed from Blizzard.

The strategic maneuvering intensified, and contradictions widened.

Although the idea of switching distributors surfaced countless times within Blizzard’s internal discussions, the company remained reluctant to act at that time. The rationale was simple: Blizzard believed that no decision should come at the expense of its players. Those familiar with Blizzard know this is the core of the company’s culture and the fundamental premise behind many of its choices.

Even on May 21, 2007, when EA acquired a 15% stake in Jiucheng for $167 million, Blizzard—EA’s major rival—remained furious yet silent.
It was not until 2008, when Blizzard had reached its breaking point, that it began courting other publishers to assess the feasibility of taking over operations itself. During this limbo, Jiucheng’s senior management continued to handle its relationship with Blizzard with obdurate inflexibility. This approach failed to ease the escalating tensions and ultimately only served to enrage Blizzard.

A word of clarification is warranted here: Jiucheng’s strategy was predicated on controlling and balancing its negotiating leverage against Blizzard. In any negotiation, both parties seek dominance. Thus, their diversification of publishing deals, attempts to break free from Blizzard’s orbit, push for self-developed products, and even the decision to bring EA into the fold were all calculated moves to increase their bargaining chips. From a strategic standpoint, this general direction was not inherently flawed.

However, the unfortunate reality is that Jiucheng was choked by the bottlenecks of its era. At that time, China’s game industry struggled to achieve independent development; indeed, many years later, one must still ask: how many games in the world can truly surpass *World of Warcraft*? It is akin to a small nation seeking independence: without nuclear deterrents, without countermeasures, lacking absolute advantages in resources or economy, and dependent on a single partner for its primary revenue, the notion of "standing on equal footing" remains little more than an empty slogan.

As Deng Xiaoping famously observed, "Backwardness invites aggression." Today, that aggression is merely more civilized. At the time, Jiucheng appeared blind to this reality, remaining stubbornly defiant. Then came the night when, after a final deliberation where Blizzard had decided not to "divorce," Activision Blizzard’s CEO Bobby Kotick suddenly issued the order to sever ties with Jiucheng once and for all.
Whether it arrived via email, in a report, or as a single sentence, the medium no longer mattered. The collision between a domineering foreign corporation and a resolute local distributor—a marriage of incompatible temperaments—was fated to end in separation.

The final straw descended slowly, yet triggered the collapse of the entire situation in an instant.

Let me briefly summarize the aftermath. After Blizzard courted numerous potential partners, NetEase and Tencent remained the finalists. Ultimately, Blizzard’s US office determined that NetEase—and its founder, Ding Lei—demonstrated an attitude toward players that better aligned with Blizzard’s core culture, securing the partnership.

On July 30, 2009, NetEase reopened closed beta testing for *World of Warcraft*.

I will save the remainder of this tale for another time.
